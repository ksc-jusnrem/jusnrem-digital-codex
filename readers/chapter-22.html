ASEAN and Emerging Regional Models

Khurram Chughtai. A standalone chapter from Code, Law and Capital: The Future of Digital Assets in Pakistan. Law and evidence stated to 22 August 2026.
ASEAN coordinates payments, data and financial policy without displacing Member-State law. Its divergence is not regulatory failure but a constitutional fact. Pakistan should learn from interoperable payment cooperation and national experimentation while resisting false harmonisation or the assumption that one country's licence travels across the region.
ABSTRACTThis chapter examines ASEAN's institutional character and regional payment connectivity, then compares Malaysia, Indonesia, Thailand, the Philippines and Vietnam, with a cross-reference to Singapore. It also places Switzerland, Liechtenstein, Canada and selected emerging-market models in a disciplined comparative frame. The chapter distinguishes cooperation, national legislation and time-limited experimentation, and derives lessons for Pakistan's regional and domestic legal design.
KEYWORDSASEAN; Malaysia; Indonesia; Thailand; Philippines; Vietnam; regional payments; regulatory divergence.
RESEARCH QUESTIONHow can regional cooperation improve digital finance when licensing, property and enforcement remain national?
PRINCIPAL INDEPENDENT CONTRIBUTIONA model of comparison that preserves national legal effect while identifying operational areas for interoperability.
JURISDICTION AND LEGAL CUT-OFFASEAN and selected comparator jurisdictions, stated to 22 August 2026.

22.1 ASEAN’s Institutional Character

22.1.1 Cooperation rather than a MiCA-style uniform legal system

#1
ASEAN works through treaties, declarations, sectoral bodies and Member-State implementation. It has not conferred EU-style legislative competence for a directly applicable cryptoasset code. Regional declarations can organise cooperation and political commitment, but market permissions and sanctions depend on national law.

22.1.2 Member-state competence and regulatory divergence

#2
Member States differ in financial-market depth, capital controls, monetary priorities, legal systems and supervisory capacity. Divergence therefore appears in token classification, licensing, custody, payment use and enforcement. An accurate regional account identifies the national instrument and authority behind each proposition.

22.2 Regional Financial Integration

22.2.1 Payments, settlement and capital-market coordination

#3
The 2023 ASEAN Leaders' Declaration supports regional payment connectivity, local-currency transactions and interoperable cross-border systems while taking country circumstances into account.1 This agenda concerns regulated payment rails and central-bank cooperation; it is not a regional permission for privately issued digital assets.

22.2.2 Cross-border data and supervisory cooperation

#4
Payment links require common messaging, identity assurance, cyber resilience, foreign-exchange handling, dispute allocation and lawful data transfer. Cooperation should specify purpose, security and confidentiality. Interoperability without liability rules can transmit operational failure as efficiently as value.

22.3 Singapore’s Regional Role

22.3.1 Institutional hub and wholesale experimentation

#5
Singapore supplies regional financial infrastructure, licensed digital-payment-token services and wholesale tokenisation initiatives. Its influence derives from MAS capacity, courts, banking connections and professional services. Pilot participation or incorporation in Singapore does not confer authority elsewhere.

22.3.2 Cross-reference to the full Singapore treatment in Chapter 20

#6
Chapter 20 explains the Payment Services Act, provider licensing and institutional tokenisation in detail. For regional purposes the key point is interface: Singapore-based providers must still satisfy each destination state's marketing, payments, securities, AML and data rules.

22.4 Malaysia

22.4.1 Securities regulation, recognised markets and digital assets

#7
Malaysia's Capital Markets and Services (Prescription of Securities) (Digital Currency and Digital Token) Order 2019 brings specified digital currencies and tokens within the Securities Commission's framework. The Commission regulates digital-asset exchanges through its Guidelines on Recognized Markets and issuance and custody through its Guidelines on Digital Assets.2 Revised exchange guidelines effective 20 May 2026 strengthen governance, financial and safeguarding requirements.3

22.4.2 Islamic finance, custody and token offerings

#8
Malaysia joins securities supervision to a developed Islamic-capital-market architecture. The Commission's Shariah Advisory Council may assess particular digital assets for trading on regulated exchanges. That status is asset- and market-specific, not a universal religious determination. Custody and token offerings retain issuer, disclosure, segregation and AML duties.

22.5 Indonesia

22.5.1 Commodity, financial-sector and payment approaches

#9
Indonesia historically treated cryptoassets as commodities for trading supervision while Bank Indonesia restricted their use as payment. Law No 4 of 2023 on Financial Sector Development and Strengthening reallocated digital financial-asset supervision toward the Financial Services Authority, OJK. Commodity classification and payment status answer different questions.

22.5.2 Transition of institutional responsibility

#10
OJK Regulation No 27 of 2024 established the digital-financial-asset trading framework following the supervisory transfer; Regulation No 23 of 2025 expanded and strengthened it, including digital-asset derivatives.4 The transition illustrates the need for transferred records, licences, staff, investigations and customer protections—not only a statutory change of regulator.

22.6 Thailand

22.6.1 Digital-asset business regulation

#11
Thailand's Emergency Decree on Digital Asset Businesses BE 2561 (2018), as amended, regulates digital-asset exchanges, brokers, dealers, fund managers, advisers and token portals through Ministry of Finance licensing and SEC supervision.5 The official English version is an unofficial translation; the Thai text controls.

22.6.2 Exchange, token, payment and supervisory policy

#12
Thailand distinguishes investment and trading services from payment policy. The 2025 amendment strengthens reach over offshore operators serving persons in Thailand and supports transaction suspension in technology-enabled crime cases.6 Extraterritorial reach still requires a statutory connecting factor and practicable cooperation.

22.7 Philippines

22.7.1 Virtual-asset service providers and payment regulation

#13
Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas Circular No 1108 of 2021 replaced the earlier virtual-currency-exchange framework with guidelines for virtual-asset service providers, including licensing, governance, AML, consumer protection and technology requirements.7 Securities or investment arrangements may additionally engage the Securities and Exchange Commission.

22.7.2 Remittances, financial inclusion and enforcement

#14
Virtual-asset services intersect with remittance and inclusion in a large cross-border labour economy. Lower transfer cost must be tested against fraud, volatility, cash-out access and recourse. Enforcement alerts and application moratoria are administrative measures with their own scope; they should not be represented as permanent statutory prohibitions.

22.8 Vietnam

22.8.1 Market use and evolving legal policy

#15
Vietnam moved from an incomplete framework to a controlled pilot for a cryptoasset market under Government Resolution No 05/2025/NQ-CP, effective 9 September 2025.8 A pilot is bounded by its resolution, eligible operators and supervisory conditions. It does not retrospectively regularise all market activity.

22.8.2 Payment restrictions and regulatory development

#16
Private cryptoassets have not thereby become lawful means of payment; payment-instrument rules and State Bank authority remain distinct. The Government's 2026–2030 programme contemplates further legislative development.9 Pakistan should read Vietnam as an example of staged experimentation, not a completed national code.

22.9 Divergence Among Member-State Models

22.9.1 Classification, licensing and enforcement

#17
Malaysia uses securities prescriptions; Indonesia separates trading from payment and transferred supervision; Thailand has a bespoke decree; the Philippines uses payment and AML supervision; Vietnam operates a pilot. All address digital assets, but their classifications, legal effects and enforcement bodies differ.

22.9.2 Why regional comparison must preserve national law

#18
A regional table can conceal the decisive facts: whether the instrument is legislation or guidance, whether it has commenced, which activity is licensed and whether the provider may serve foreign customers. National law must remain visible in every comparison and cross-border contract.

22.10 Cross-Border Payments and Regional Settlement

22.10.1 Interoperability and currency arrangements

#19
QR linkages and fast-payment connections can reduce remittance cost without using a privately issued token. Their legal foundations include settlement finality, access criteria, foreign-exchange rules, scheme liability and central-bank cooperation. Technical connection is the last mile of a legal and institutional arrangement.

22.10.2 Private digital assets and central-bank initiatives

#20
Private stablecoins, tokenised deposits and CBDCs allocate credit, redemption and governance differently. Regional initiatives should test which claim moves across the interface and which institution bears failure. “Interoperable” should never mean that these liabilities are legally identical.

22.11 Other Significant Comparative Models

22.11.1 Switzerland, Liechtenstein and Canada

#21
Switzerland applies financial-market law and its DLT legislative amendments, including DLT trading facilities and ledger-based securities; FINMA supervises according to activity. Liechtenstein's Token and Trusted Technology Service Provider Act supplies a civil and supervisory framework. Canada applies provincial securities law and national AML rules, with platform registration coordinated through the Canadian Securities Administrators.10

22.11.2 Africa, Latin America and relevant offshore financial centres

#22
El Salvador's legal-tender experiment, Brazil's virtual-asset legislation, South Africa's financial-product classification and offshore licensing centres illustrate different policy objectives. Small-state or offshore models may rely on foreign markets and external enforcement. Headline adoption should not be treated as evidence of inclusion, stability or institutional success.

22.12 Lessons for Pakistan

22.12.1 Regional cooperation without false harmonisation

#23
Pakistan can pursue bilateral and regional payment links, AML cooperation, regulator-to-regulator assistance and common technical messaging without pretending that national laws are unified. Each arrangement should preserve domestic authority, data safeguards, liability and review.

22.12.2 Market, institutional and legal-design choices

#24
The ASEAN experience favours staged entry, public registers, activity-specific duties and managed institutional transfer. Pakistan's present task is to operationalise its Act and notified Regulations, not imitate a foreign label. Any pilot must state its legal basis, limits, customer protections and exit path.
#25
Regional payment connectivity provides a concrete model of cooperation without harmonised asset law. Central banks can link fast-payment systems, agree messages and foreign-exchange treatment, manage settlement and allocate operational responsibility while each state retains its currency and licensing rules. Pakistan can pursue comparable links through SBP and competent counterparts. A private stablecoin is not required for that objective, and should not be inserted into a public payment link without separate issuer, reserve and redemption authority.
#26
Supervisory cooperation can follow the same pattern. A memorandum may establish contacts, consultation and information exchange, but each authority acts under its own law. Requests should identify purpose, permitted use, confidentiality and onward disclosure. Joint exercises can test cyber incidents, provider failure and customer transfer. Such practical cooperation is more credible than a declaration of common standards unsupported by compatible procedures.
#27
The Indonesia transition is particularly relevant to Pakistan's institutional design. Moving supervision from a commodity authority to OJK required more than new legal text: licences, records, staff, investigations and market infrastructure had to move or be recognised. Pakistan's current challenge involves coordination rather than the same transfer, but the operational lesson holds. A provision assigning responsibility should be accompanied by data, personnel, procedure, budget and a tested handoff.
#28
Malaysia illustrates how a capital-markets regulator can combine exchange, issuance and custody rules with Islamic-finance governance. Yet the Securities Commission acts under Malaysia's statutes and institutional history. Pakistan should not import a Malaysian Shariah classification as a market permission. It can require transparent product review, competence, conflicts management and ongoing monitoring while maintaining PVARA, SBP and SECP responsibility for the legal and financial consequences.
#29
Thailand's 2025 extraterritorial amendment addresses offshore providers serving persons in Thailand. Pakistan likewise needs a connecting rule for remote providers. The rule should identify targeted marketing, local onboarding, payment channels, language, customer volume and other evidence of service into Pakistan. Worldwide website accessibility is too broad; voluntary evasion of a clear domestic customer connection is too narrow. Enforcement should combine local conditions with international cooperation and app or payment interventions authorised by law.
#30
The Philippines and Vietnam show different uses of staged control. BSP's VASP framework builds on payment and remittance supervision, while Vietnam's pilot is a legally bounded experiment. Pakistan already has an Act and Regulations, so a nationwide pilot label would obscure the present baseline. It may still use sandboxes for a novel activity if the ordinary law identifies which requirements can be varied and how customers are protected.

#31
The non-ASEAN comparators reinforce the point. Switzerland and Liechtenstein pair innovative statutory categories with established courts and financial supervision. Canada shows coordinated securities regulation without one national securities statute. Brazil, South Africa and other emerging markets adapt existing payment or financial-product law. An offshore centre may achieve fast incorporation but rely on foreign banks and enforcement. Pakistan should compare the complete institutional package and failure route, not the novelty of the statutory vocabulary.
#32
This approach changes how “regional leadership” should be measured. The relevant indicators are cost and speed of lawful payments, clarity of market permissions, recoverability of customer assets, effectiveness of cooperation, cybersecurity and access to remedy. Volume of token trading or the number of memoranda does not establish those outcomes. Regional initiatives should publish evidence, limits and revision dates.
#33
Pakistan's broader diplomatic position can then be coherent. It can support interoperable payments and tax or AML cooperation, recognise foreign permissions conditionally, and preserve domestic customer and monetary law. This is neither isolation nor false harmonisation. It is an architecture in which cross-border functions are connected while public authority remains traceable to the relevant state.
#34
Regional data cooperation requires an additional safeguard. Identity and transaction information collected for a payment, AML or tax purpose should not be transferred for undefined innovation use. Agreements should identify the controller or custodian, accuracy process, security, retention, correction and breach response. Pakistan should establish these protections in domestic law before promising seamless supervisory data exchange.
#35
Small and medium providers also matter. Capital and technology requirements can improve resilience while entrenching a few regional groups. Malaysia's 2026 strengthening of DAX governance and safeguarding illustrates the need to connect higher requirements to identified risks. Pakistan should assess proportionality, allow shared services under controlled outsourcing and monitor concentration, without lowering customer-asset or integrity standards.
#36
An ASEAN-style model of incremental connectivity may therefore suit Pakistan better than an ambition for immediate comprehensive regional harmonisation. Begin with defined payment corridors, regulator contacts and evidence standards; test them; publish outcomes; then expand. Each new connection should identify the domestic rule it depends on and the event that would suspend it. Regionalism becomes credible when it is operationally narrow and legally complete.
#37
Consumer redress must travel with connectivity. A user should know which provider received the instruction, when settlement became final, how foreign exchange was priced, who handles error and which forum hears the complaint. Regional payment links can allocate these questions through scheme rules and domestic law. Private-token arrangements need equivalent clarity about issuer, reserve, custodian and redemption. Lower cost without a recoverable claim is not durable inclusion.
#38
Pakistan should also distinguish recognition from reliance. It may recognise that a foreign provider meets specified home-state outcomes and still require a local representative, disclosures, data access and customer-asset conditions. It may rely on a home supervisor for part of the examination while retaining the power to restrict local service. The decision should be written, activity-specific, reviewable and capable of withdrawal.
#39
These controls make diversity an asset. ASEAN Member States can test different classifications and supervisory methods while sharing operational lessons. Pakistan can participate in regional learning without importing a whole regime or understating its own current law. Comparative material becomes a disciplined source of options, and domestic institutions remain responsible for the choice.
#40
Future comparison should record whether initiatives produce accessible and recoverable services. A payment connection that works only for large banks, a licence that excludes smaller lawful firms without risk justification, or a pilot without a route to ordinary law may not advance inclusion. Pakistan should place distributional evidence beside technical performance and market volume when deciding whether to extend a regional arrangement.

Chapter 22 Conclusion

#41
ASEAN's achievement is practical cooperation amid legal diversity. Its payment-connectivity work shows that states can make systems interoperable while retaining monetary sovereignty. Its national frameworks show that transition and institutional allocation are substantive legal work. Pakistan can pursue both lessons without claiming a regional passport or completed harmonisation.
#42
National instruments and official-language texts control; translations and regional declarations should be used according to their legal status.

Chapter 22 — Law as Code, Publication and the Rule Register

22.1 The publication failure is the operational failure

Most of the [GAP] findings in this report are, at bottom, publication failures. An instrument that exists in draft, or that exists but was never authenticated and gazetted, or that exists but cannot be located by the person it binds, does not regulate. The remedy is unglamorous and cheap: publish properly, and publish in a form a machine can read.

22.2 The rule register

Every regulator that makes binding instruments should maintain a public register recording, for each instrument: its title and number; its parent power in the primary legislation; the date of making, publication and commencement; its current status; every amendment with dates; and a permanent link to the authenticated text.

Pakistan does not have this for virtual assets, and its absence is precisely why this report has had to flag so many instruments as unverified. A register is a week of work and it eliminates an entire category of legal risk.

22.3 Authentication and the limits of handbooks

[OPERATIVE] The Act distinguishes rules made by the Federal Government from regulations made by the Authority.c22-1 Both are subordinate legislation with parent-power, publication and commencement requirements. A handbook, a guideline, a circular, a frequently-asked-questions page and an application form are not in that category unless the parent statute says so.

The consequence is one that firms should be told directly: an obligation that exists only in a handbook is vulnerable to challenge as being outside the rule-making power, and a penalty imposed for breaching it is vulnerable to being set aside in constitutional review. That is not a reason for firms to ignore handbooks — regulators have other tools — but it is a reason for the Authority to convert its handbook content into properly made regulations before enforcing it.

[UNVERIFIED] The superseded drafts cited a constitutional authority for the proposition that un-gazetted instruments are nullities, and attributed it to the wrong court. The proposition is orthodox and can be supported; the citation must be replaced.c22-2

22.4 Rules as code

Machine-readability is not a technology project. It means publishing, alongside the authoritative text, a structured representation of the obligations the text creates: who is bound, on what trigger, by what deadline, to what threshold, with what evidence.

Four instruments in this framework are natural first candidates because they are threshold-and-schema based rather than judgement-based: the travel-rule data requirement, once made; customer-asset reconciliation and reporting; the licensing application taxonomy against the ten Schedule I categories; and incident-reporting timelines. Publishing those as schemas would let a provider build compliance directly against the instrument and let the supervisor test compliance automatically.

Two disciplines are essential. The authoritative instrument remains the published legal text; the machine-readable artefact is derivative and must say so. And the derivative must be versioned in lockstep with amendments, or it becomes a trap.

22.5 Policy and strategy, distinguished

Pakistan needs two documents it does not have, and they are different in kind.

A national policy on distributed-ledger technology states durable principles and boundaries: national objectives; constitutional competence and the federal–provincial allocation; a mandatory alternatives test requiring a demonstration that a ledger is superior to a conventional database for the use case; legal recognition of records, signatures and automated execution; the rules for choosing between public and permissioned networks; open standards and vendor-lock avoidance; identity, privacy and localisation alignment; intellectual property and source-code access; validator, node and oracle governance; environmental, energy and consumer boundaries; evidential and archival auditability; monetary sovereignty and the foreign-exchange interface; pilot and migration safeguards; skills and research; and parliamentary transparency.

A national strategy is costed delivery: a baseline of existing projects; a ranked public use-case pipeline; named lead institutions; a technical reference architecture; budget and procurement pathways; stage-gates from concept to production; explicit stop, redesign and exit criteria; incident response and key management; federal and provincial milestones; outcome-based indicators; a public project register; and a sequenced roadmap.

The distinction matters because Pakistan enacted the licensing statute before either existed. That is the reverse of the sequence followed in the comparator jurisdictions, and it is the structural reason the subordinate instruments have been slow: they are being drafted without a settled policy to be drafted against.
