Chapter Summary

#1
This chapter maps the different legal effects of FATF, FSB, Basel Committee, CPMI, IOSCO, IMF, World Bank, OECD and UNIDROIT materials, together with accounting standards and supervisory cooperation. It explains how soft law can become operationally consequential without itself becoming Pakistani legislation. The chapter offers Pakistan a method for reasoned incorporation: identify the standard, domestic legal vehicle, competent institution, affected persons, evidence, safeguards and review.

23.1 Hard Law, Soft Law and Regulatory Standards

23.1.1 Different sources and different legal effects

#2
Treaties, Security Council decisions, domestic statutes, regulations, institutional recommendations, technical principles and industry standards are not interchangeable. A treaty binds according to international law and domestic implementation; a regulator's rule binds through delegated authority; a standard may guide policy or assessment without directly creating an offence, tax or licence condition.

23.1.2 Domestic incorporation, evidence and institutional influence

#3
Soft law becomes consequential through legislation, licence conditions, supervisory expectations, correspondent relationships, market practice and external assessment. That influence can be intense while remaining legally mediated. Every domestic use should identify its enabling provision, scope, addressee and route of challenge.

23.1.3 Comparative Method and Selection Criteria

#4
Pakistan’s first question is methodological: what must be true before a foreign rule can support a domestic recommendation? Seven matters must be recorded. The comparison must identify: the Pakistani problem; the foreign instrument’s legal force; its institutional setting; the exact rule or outcome; Pakistan’s present baseline; the resulting delta; and the condition on which transfer would work. If one is missing, the comparison is descriptive rather than corrective.
#5
This method distinguishes four kinds of material. A standard expresses an internationally agreed outcome but ordinarily requires domestic implementation. Guidance explains or supports implementation and must not be represented as binding text. A foreign enactment or rulebook binds within its own jurisdiction, not Pakistan. An implementation review reports what jurisdictions have done and may expose recurrent failure; it does not silently enlarge the underlying standard. Dates matter because a 2025 implementation review can qualify confidence in a 2023 framework without amending its text.
#6
Comparators are selected by function, not prestige. FATF is used because Chapter 23 identified an enacted AML and Travel Rule foundation whose supervisory allocation and operating specification remain incomplete. FSB and IOSCO are used because Chapters 9, 10, 15 and 16 identified cross-regulator, multi-function, client-asset, market-abuse and enforcement questions. MiCA is used because Pakistan has enacted issuer and service-provider regimes but must complete their taxonomy and operating detail. CARF and DAC8 are used because Chapter 23 identified a tax-information problem distinct from licensing. VARA is used for instrument architecture. Hong Kong is used for platform terminology, token admission and custody controls. South Africa is used to test the narrower technique of adapting existing law. The question is never which jurisdiction is “best”; it is which design feature answers a proved Pakistani problem at acceptable legal and institutional cost.1
#7
Three limits follow. First, similarity of words does not establish similarity of law. “VASP”, “CASP”, “VATP” and “licensed corporation” belong to different statutory settings. Secondly, a rule cannot be detached from the institution that administers it. EU passporting depends on the Union’s internal-market structure; Dubai’s rulebooks operate within VARA’s territorial mandate; Hong Kong’s platform regime connects to the SFC’s securities architecture. Thirdly, comparison does not decide constitutional competence, delegated authority, property character, tax liability or appellate jurisdiction in Pakistan. Those questions remain governed by Pakistani law.

23.2 Financial Action Task Force

23.2.1 Virtual assets, VASPs and risk-based supervision

#8
FATF Recommendation 15 and its Interpretive Note require countries to assess virtual-asset risks and regulate or prohibit virtual-asset service providers consistently with the risk-based approach.2 The definitions are functional and designed for financial integrity. They do not determine private ownership, tax character or securities status.

23.2.2 Travel Rule, beneficial ownership and proliferation financing

#9
FATF expects covered providers to obtain, hold and transmit specified originator and beneficiary information and to apply beneficial-ownership, targeted-financial-sanctions and proliferation-financing controls. Implementation must preserve data security, legal purpose and proportionality. The standard does not itself authorise indiscriminate surveillance or create a domestic offence.

23.2.3 FATF: From Statutory Recognition to Operational Financial Integrity

#10
Pakistan’s FATF question is: does the present order translate Recommendation 15 into an identifiable, supervised and testable system for every relevant VASP function, including offshore-facing and technology-mediated arrangements? The FATF Recommendations, as amended in June 2026, require countries to assess virtual-asset risk, license or register VASPs, apply preventive measures and effective supervision, and provide proportionate and dissuasive sanctions.3
#11
Pakistan’s baseline is now statutory and regulatory. The Virtual Assets Act 2026 (Act XIII of 2026) deems licensed VASPs to be financial institutions for Anti-Money Laundering Act 2010 purposes, imposes specified AML/CFT/CPF duties on VASPs and issuers, and requires originator and beneficiary information for transfers at or above a threshold prescribed by PVARA. The Pakistan Virtual Asset Services Regulations 2026 were notified on 21 August 2026 as SRO 1419(I)/2026; regulation 100 fixes the Travel Rule threshold at the PKR equivalent of USD 1,000 and specifies core information and control requirements. The remaining delta is operational: supervisory allocation, goAML onboarding, secure messaging, aggregation and exception handling, counterparty controls, inspections, sanctions, appeals and evidence of effectiveness must work in practice.4
#12
Pakistan’s second FATF question is: which actors fall within the functional perimeter when a business presents itself as software, DeFi or a protocol rather than a conventional company? FATF’s 2021 guidance and July 2026 DeFi report direct attention to activities actually performed and to whether a natural or legal person exercises control or sufficient influence. The domestic transfer condition remains a fact-and-control test under the enacted VASP definition. The regulatory file should identify persons who operate the business, retain administrative or upgrade power, control interfaces or fees, select counterparties, custody assets or exercise sufficient influence over performance. VASP status should not be inferred from publication of code, token ownership or participation alone.5
#13
Pakistan’s third FATF question is: does enactment equal effectiveness? FATF’s seventh targeted update of 16 July 2026 reports further progress but continuing gaps in converting risk assessments into mitigation, operationalising licensing or registration, identifying actors and conducting effective risk-based supervision and enforcement. Its design lesson is not a league table; a legal rule is incomplete as a control until entities, supervisors, systems, tests, findings and remediation can be evidenced.6
#14
For Pakistan, operational proof should include: a current public register tied to the confidential AML reporting population; entity-level goAML onboarding; a Travel Rule conformance suite; inspections of actual messages and exceptions; a process for foreign and unlicensed VASPs targeting Pakistan; documented risk treatment for self-hosted wallets; and aggregate effectiveness reporting. Notification of a regulation proves that an instrument was issued; it does not prove that the regulated population, supervisory systems or enforcement controls are functioning.
#15
Pakistan should use FATF as the outcome baseline and maintain an authenticated AMLA–VAA–PVARA allocation showing who supervises each obligation, which data may move, how decisions are challenged and what evidence demonstrates effectiveness. The June and July 2026 FATF materials should be version-controlled rather than cited through an older implementation snapshot.

23.3 Financial Stability Board

23.3.1 Global regulatory framework and systemic risk

#16
The FSB's 2023 global framework contains high-level recommendations for cryptoasset activities and global stablecoins, built around “same activity, same risk, same regulation”.7 It emphasises governance, data, risk management, cross-border cooperation and effective resolution. The formula requires functional analysis; it does not mean that unlike legal claims should receive identical rules.

23.3.2 Cross-border consistency and implementation review

#17
FSB implementation reviews compare whether jurisdictions have converted recommendations into effective frameworks. Pakistan should use the comparison to identify gaps, publish its response to each recommendation and explain adoption, adaptation or rejection.

23.3.3 FSB and IOSCO: Systemic, Market and Multi-Function Deltas

#18
Pakistan’s FSB question is: can the regulatory perimeter follow economic function and financial-system connection when one group combines exchange, custody, lending, settlement, token issuance and proprietary activity? The FSB’s 2023 high-level recommendations are a global financial-stability baseline. They call for adequate powers and resources; comprehensive, functional and proportionate regulation; domestic and cross-border cooperation; governance; risk management; data and disclosure; monitoring of interconnections; and treatment of multi-function service providers. The FSB expressly limits its own mandate: the recommendations do not comprehensively cover AML/CFT, privacy, cybersecurity, consumer protection, market integrity, competition, tax, monetary policy or monetary sovereignty.8
#19
Pakistan’s baseline includes an activity schedule, licensing powers, fit-and-proper review, customer and issuer duties, prudential delegation, data reporting and inter-agency provisions. The delta lies in the connections. PVARA’s licence cannot displace SBP’s payment, banking, currency or foreign-exchange functions, or SECP’s securities, company and futures jurisdiction. A combined group may create risks not visible in an activity-by-activity form: use of customer assets to support proprietary positions; common technology and wallet infrastructure; intra-group lending; correlated reserves; conflicts in token listing; and contagion through a shared treasury. Pakistan should require a consolidated function and dependency map, related-entity disclosure, common-risk assessment, restrictions or separation where conflicts cannot be managed, and shared supervisory products owned by the legally competent institutions.
#20
Pakistan’s implementation question is: how much confidence should be placed in announced frameworks before supervisory performance is visible? The FSB’s 2025 thematic review found progress but significant gaps and inconsistencies, with less progress for global stablecoin arrangements and continuing cross-border difficulties. It assessed framework implementation as of August 2025, not the effectiveness of every regime. The lesson supports Chapter 23’s staged readiness test: published law, appointed institutions, valid instruments, connected data, trained supervisors, completed examinations, remediation and measured outcomes are separate states.9
#21
Pakistan’s IOSCO question is: what market outcomes must a virtual-asset regime reproduce where activity is substitutable for securities-market intermediation? IOSCO’s 2023 final report contains eighteen recommendations organised around conflicts from vertical integration, market manipulation and fraud, custody and client assets, cross-border cooperation, operational and technological risk, and retail distribution. It seeks regulatory outcomes consistent with those applied in traditional financial markets, while allowing jurisdictions to use their own legal architecture.10
#22
Pakistan’s baseline addresses fair dealing, conflicts, market manipulation, insider trading, custody, complaints, cyber controls and sanctions. The delta is specification and supervision. A platform should have documented token-admission criteria, surveillance ownership, proprietary-trading controls, order and price integrity rules, conflict disclosures, customer categorisation, custody reconciliations, incident escalation and a complaint-to-remedy path. Where a token or activity is already a security or futures product, the answer is not a parallel PVARA version of securities law. It is classification, referral or coordinated supervision under the competent statute.
#23
Pakistan’s final IOSCO question is: does formal coverage show effective market supervision? IOSCO’s 2025 thematic review recorded meaningful implementation progress among participating jurisdictions but did not assess the effectiveness of application across authorisation, supervision and enforcement. Pakistan should therefore avoid claiming IOSCO alignment from legislative text alone. Alignment should be demonstrated through tested market surveillance, custody examinations, conflict remediation, enforcement reasons and cross-border cooperation outcomes.11

23.4 BIS and the Basel Committee

23.4.1 Bank exposure and prudential treatment

#24
The Basel Committee's prudential standard classifies bank cryptoasset exposures into groups with differing capital treatment and imposes infrastructure, redemption and exposure conditions.12 It is addressed to bank supervision, not to the legality of public trading. Domestic effect comes through SBP's lawful adoption within Pakistan's banking framework.

23.4.2 Tokenised deposits, stablecoins and market infrastructure

#25
BIS work distinguishes tokenised deposits, stablecoins and central-bank money by issuer liability, settlement asset and governance. Tokenising a deposit should not erase the bank's obligation or depositor protection. Cross-system arrangements must preserve finality and the singleness of money where that is the policy objective.

23.5 Committee on Payments and Market Infrastructures

23.5.1 Payment and settlement principles

#26
The Principles for Financial Market Infrastructures organise governance, credit, liquidity, settlement, custody, operational and access risks.13 They apply to designated systemically important arrangements through the relevant legal and supervisory framework. A network's technical decentralisation does not answer whether it performs an FMI function.

23.5.2 Stablecoin and cross-border payment arrangements

#27
CPMI-IOSCO guidance confirms that a systemically important stablecoin arrangement should observe relevant PFMIs.14 The arrangement includes governance, transfer, settlement and reserve functions, not only the issuer. For Pakistan, designation and oversight must rest on statute and transparent criteria.

23.6 International Organization of Securities Commissions

23.6.1 Market integrity and investor protection

#28
IOSCO's 2023 recommendations address regulatory outcomes for trading, custody, conflicts, disclosure, market abuse, retail distribution and cross-border cooperation.15 They are designed for consistent outcomes while allowing domestic legal forms. They do not decide whether a particular token is a security under Pakistani law.

23.6.2 DeFi, conflicts and multi-function platforms

#29
IOSCO's DeFi recommendations focus on responsible persons and economically equivalent functions.16 Multi-function platforms create conflicts where listing, trading, proprietary dealing, custody and lending are combined. Pakistan can respond through separation, disclosure, governance and capital, but must identify who is legally responsible and why.

23.7 International Monetary Fund and World Bank

23.7.1 Monetary, fiscal and institutional assessment

#30
The IMF-FSB Synthesis Paper and IMF policy elements examine macro-financial stability, monetary sovereignty, capital-flow management, tax and institutional capacity.17 Their analytical value is greatest where digital assets interact with external accounts and domestic payment confidence. They are not domestic prohibitions or licences.

23.7.2 Technical assistance, conditionality and development

#31
Technical assistance can strengthen legislation, data and supervision. Programme conditionality, where agreed under an IMF arrangement, has a different source and effect from a general staff paper. World Bank development work adds inclusion, infrastructure and institutional perspectives. Pakistan should disclose which proposition comes from which instrument.

23.8 OECD and Crypto-Asset Reporting Framework

23.8.1 Tax transparency and reporting

#32
The OECD Crypto-Asset Reporting Framework identifies reporting cryptoasset service providers, relevant cryptoassets, reportable users and transactions for exchange of tax information.18 Its tax-transparency taxonomy is purpose-specific. It should not silently determine PVARA licensing or private-law ownership.

23.8.2 Implementation, data exchange and legal limits

#33
Implementation requires domestic legislation, competent-authority arrangements, due diligence, data safeguards and information-exchange infrastructure. A reporting obligation should specify persons, fields, valuation rules, retention and remedies. International exchange must operate within applicable confidentiality and taxpayer protections.

23.8.3 CARF and DAC8: Tax Transparency Is Not Market Licensing

#34
Pakistan’s CARF question is: what legal and technical chain would allow FBR to receive and, where lawfully arranged, exchange reliable tax information on crypto-asset transactions? The OECD’s CARF is an international tax-transparency framework. Its three components are model rules and commentary for domestic transposition, a multilateral competent authority agreement or other exchange arrangements, and an XML schema for reporting and exchange. Its four substantive building blocks concern relevant crypto-assets, reporting service providers, reportable transactions and information, and due diligence to identify users, controlling persons and tax residence.19
#35
Pakistan’s baseline is section 66 of the VAA, the Income Tax Ordinance and FBR’s existing tax-administration powers analysed in Chapter 23. A VASP licence can identify a regulated population and produce data, but it does not by itself create a CARF reporting nexus, define reportable users and controlling persons, require tax-residence self-certification, identify reportable transaction classes, establish valuation and aggregation, authorise international exchange, or satisfy confidentiality and data-safeguard requirements. Those are tax-law and administrative-cooperation questions. PVARA may supply a verified licensee list and lawfully held data; FBR must own the tax-reporting rule and competent-authority chain.
#36
Pakistan’s implementation-status question is: has an international CARF commitment already supplied the missing legal basis? On the OECD commitments list last updated 23 June 2026, Pakistan is not shown among jurisdictions committed to commence CARF exchanges in 2027, 2028 or 2029; it is also absent from the CARF-MCAA signatories list stated to be current as of 3 March 2026. Absence from those lists does not prohibit Pakistan from adopting domestic reporting or later committing. It means this chapter must not state that Pakistan has made a CARF exchange commitment or that the CARF-MCAA presently authorises exchange for Pakistan.20
#37
Pakistan’s DAC8 question is: what can a binding EU implementation model reveal about the domestic machinery CARF requires? DAC8 amends the EU administrative-cooperation regime. It requires reporting and due diligence by reporting crypto-asset service providers, information on reportable users and controlling persons, annual reporting, records, compliance procedures and exchange between tax administrations. It also reaches defined crypto-asset operators outside MiCA’s authorisation population through a registration architecture. Its first reporting period begins from 1 January 2026. DAC8 expressly operates as tax administration; MiCA authorisation data feeds it but does not replace it.21
#38
The transferable lesson is a separate tax-information lane connected to, but not absorbed by, PVARA licensing. Pakistan would need primary authority or valid tax rules for nexus, due diligence, fields, reportable transactions, valuation, corrections, retention, inspection and sanctions; a PVARA–FBR data gateway with purpose and access controls; secure schema testing; and an international legal basis before exchange. The non-transferable features are EU member-state allocation, single registration and automatic exchange under Union law. The chapter therefore sequences feasibility, domestic legislation, safeguards, technical readiness and any commitment decision. Chapter 23 does not recommend declaring CARF operational by press release.

23.9 UNIDROIT Principles on Digital Assets and Private Law

23.9.1 Control, custody and transfer

#39
The 2023 UNIDROIT Principles address digital assets, control, custody, innocent acquisition, secured transactions, enforcement and insolvency.22 They offer a coherent vocabulary for private-law reform and technology-neutral connecting rules. They are principles, not a self-executing global property code.

23.9.2 Use as persuasive principles rather than domestic enactment

#40
Pakistani courts and drafters may use the Principles as persuasive comparative material where consistent with domestic law. Binding rules on priority, insolvency or third-party effects require valid enactment or judicial development within authorised doctrine. Citation should never be represented as incorporation.

23.10 Accounting and Prudential Standards

23.10.1 Recognition, measurement and disclosure

#41
The IFRS Interpretations Committee concluded in 2019 that holdings of cryptocurrencies generally fall within IAS 2 when held for sale in the ordinary course of business and otherwise within IAS 38, on the facts described.23 That agenda decision addresses accounting, not legal ownership or regulatory status. New contractual token rights may require separate analysis.

23.10.2 Institutional exposure and comparability

#42
Comparable accounts require valuation policies, control evidence, liability recognition and disclosures about concentration and custody. Prudential supervisors may impose treatment beyond accounting measurement. Audit evidence must address keys, rights and obligations, not merely inspect a public balance.

23.11 Supervisory Colleges and Information Exchange

23.11.1 Cross-border coordination

#43
Supervisory colleges and memoranda can coordinate group risk, licensing changes, cyber incidents and failure planning. They do not merge regulators or transfer coercive power by implication. The home and host authority, legal gateways and decision rights must remain clear.

23.11.2 Confidentiality, due process and accountability

#44
Information exchange should have a defined purpose, security classification, retention rule and correction process. Adverse action should rest on disclosable reasons or lawful protected procedures. Confidential cooperation cannot become an unreviewable blacklist.

23.12 Convergence, Fragmentation and Legal Effect in Pakistan

23.12.1 Standards as evidence, influence and conditionality

#45
Standards can evidence recognised risk, inform proportionality and shape external market access. Pakistan’s Virtual Assets Act 2026 and PVARA regulations notified on 21 August 2026 refer to international standards within their respective statutory and delegated scope. Regulators should publish a concordance showing the exact domestic instrument and any national adaptation.

23.12.2 Domestic concordance, implementation evidence and revision control

#46
The operative publication question is which Pakistani instrument gives a particular international proposition legal effect. A domestic concordance should record the originating body and version, outcome sought, adopt/adapt/reject decision, competent institution, enabling provision, instrument, commencement, affected persons, safeguards, implementation evidence and review date. This prevents an examination practice, reporting form or software field from silently extending the approved rule.

#49
Standards interact but use different taxonomies. A VASP under FATF, a cryptoasset service provider under MiCA, a reporting provider under CARF and a prudential exposure under Basel are not necessarily coextensive. Verified data may be reused where lawful, but the purpose and boundary of each category must remain visible. A universal provider flag can produce both over-reporting and gaps.
#50
Supervisory colleges should exchange facts before conclusions. They may share group structure, custody chain, incidents and risk controls under lawful gateways. Each regulator then applies its own statute and gives reasons. Where confidential foreign information cannot be disclosed to the affected firm, domestic procedure should still provide the substance necessary for a meaningful answer unless a lawful exception applies.
#51
Standards require revision control. FATF Recommendations, Basel treatment and technical reporting formats change. Domestic incorporation should specify whether later versions apply automatically; open-ended ambulatory incorporation may raise delegation and notice concerns. For coercive rules, the safer method is a reasoned domestic amendment with a stated effective date, transition and historical archive.
#52
Implementation evidence should be matched to purpose. FATF effectiveness cannot be proved by counting registrations alone; Basel compliance cannot be proved by a policy if exposure data are unreliable; IOSCO outcomes cannot be proved by the number of warnings; CARF readiness cannot be proved by a data schema without lawful reporting and exchange. Pakistan’s reports should distinguish instrument issued, capability tested, live outcome and unresolved limitation.
#53
Named officers in PVARA, SBP, SECP, FMU and FBR should own standards relevant to their mandates; a coordination process should maintain the concordance and identify overlaps; and each body should make and defend its own instrument. Parliamentary and public reporting should identify the recommendation, domestic rule, responsible institution, implementation evidence, limitation and planned revision. Pakistan may adopt an outcome through a different instrument, defer it for capacity reasons or reject a technique that conflicts with domestic law, provided the disposition is reasoned, public and lawful.

Chapter 23 Conclusion

#54
Transnational standards coordinate; they do not replace domestic law. Pakistan should implement selected outcomes lawfully and recheck every later use.

Chapter 23 — Sequenced reform programme

Immediate, 0-90 day, 3-12 month and structural actions, each with owner, instrument, dependency, deadline, completion evidence and KPI. The structural programme will include adoption of a National Blockchain Policy and a separately approved National Blockchain Strategy and implementation roadmap.

National Blockchain Policy and Strategy recommendation

The report will recommend that Pakistan adopt two connected but legally and administratively distinct instruments:

A. National Blockchain Policy

The Policy should establish the State's durable principles and boundaries for distributed-ledger use. It should address:

1. National objectives and a precise statement of problems blockchain may legitimately address.

2. Constitutional competence, institutional ownership and federal-provincial coordination.

3. Technology neutrality and a mandatory alternatives test: blockchain must not be selected where a conventional database, registry or payment system is safer, cheaper and more effective.

4. Legal recognition of records, control, signatures, smart-contract execution and interaction with authoritative registries, without declaring code to be law.

5. Public, permissioned, consortium and hybrid network choices and the conditions for each.

6. Interoperability, open standards, portability and avoidance of proprietary or vendor lock-in.

7. Digital identity, privacy, data localisation, cybersecurity, cryptographic standards and key governance.

8. Procurement, intellectual-property ownership, source-code access, audit rights and conflicts safeguards.

9. Governance of validators, nodes, upgrades, emergency powers, oracles and cross-chain bridges.

10. Consumer, competition, accessibility, inclusion and environmental safeguards.

11. Evidentiary, archival, audit, records-management and judicial-enforcement requirements.

12. Relationship with payments, CBDC, tokenised deposits, securities, virtual assets, tax, foreign exchange and public finance.

13. Rules for public-sector pilots, regulatory sandboxes, discontinuation and migration.

14. Research, skills, university, standards-body and domestic technical-capacity development.

15. Transparency, parliamentary accountability, independent evaluation and publication of failures as well as successes.

B. National Blockchain Strategy

The Strategy should translate the Policy into a costed and sequenced delivery programme. It should contain:

1. A verified national baseline of existing systems, capabilities, laws, projects and infrastructure.

2. A ranked use-case pipeline based on public value, legal readiness, institutional capability, cost, risk and measurable benefit.

3. Named lead and supporting institutions for every initiative.

4. Required legislation, rules, standards, procurement and data-sharing instruments.

5. Technical reference architecture and interoperability standards.

6. Budget, funding source, procurement route, staffing and capability plan.

7. Pilot design with control groups or credible baselines where measurable impact is claimed.

8. Stage gates: concept, legal validation, technical prototype, controlled pilot, independent evaluation, production decision and scaled operation.

9. Stop, redesign and exit criteria for projects that do not demonstrate value.

10. Cybersecurity, resilience, recovery, continuity and vendor-exit arrangements.

11. Federal, provincial and local-government implementation pathways.

12. Private-sector, academic and civil-society participation under transparent conflicts and procurement rules.

13. Outcome KPIs, not activity counts: cost and time reduction, error/fraud reduction, settlement performance, service access, interoperability, revenue or verified productivity.

14. Public project register showing owner, purpose, legal basis, budget, vendor, network, stage, audit and measured result.

15. A 2027-2030 implementation roadmap with annual independent review and policy refresh triggers.

Candidate use-case screen

The Strategy should evaluate—not presume—the suitability of blockchain for land and asset registries, trade and transferable records, supply chains, public procurement, identity and credentials, securities and sukuk, payments and settlement, remittances, energy markets, carbon or environmental records, intellectual property, health or education credentials, public grants and audit trails. No use case should proceed merely because it can be tokenised.

Institutional design question

The report will determine whether policy ownership should sit with the Federal Government through a formally designated lead division and intergovernmental council, while sector regulators retain their statutory mandates. PVARA should not automatically become the regulator of every blockchain application merely because a distributed ledger is used.

Comparative sequencing thesis: did Pakistan proceed in reverse order?

The report will test the following proposition rather than state it rhetorically:

> Pakistan did not begin without policy thought, but it appears to have enacted a dedicated virtual-assets regulator and licensing statute before completing and publicly authenticating the broader National Blockchain Policy, costed National Blockchain Strategy, technical reference architecture and cross-sector implementation plan needed to govern blockchain beyond regulated virtual-asset services.

The sequence presently evidenced for Pakistan is:

1. Regulator warnings and restrictions, including the 2018 SBP instruments.

2. SECP's 2020 position paper on digital-asset trading platforms.

3. Domestic research and legislative-policy work, including the KSC 2021 corpus.

4. MoITT's later statement that a Blockchain/DLT Policy was in consultation, without a final adopted policy located in the controlled corpus.

5. Executive initiatives and the 2025 Pakistan Crypto Council/digital-assets announcements.

6. Virtual Assets Ordinance, 2025.

7. NOC and interim institutional machinery.

8. Virtual Assets Act, 2026.

9. Draft VASP Regulations and handbooks and limited banking access.

10. Continuing absence, at the cut-off, of a publicly authenticated final National Blockchain Policy and costed National Blockchain Strategy covering the wider technology and public-sector ecosystem.

This is not accurately described as “law without any preceding policy.” It is better described as sectoral virtual-assets law before completion of the overarching national blockchain policy-strategy-architecture cycle.

23.6 What would count as success

Not a licence count. Not an announcement. The measure is whether a competent applicant, on a defined date, can identify the licence, submit a complete application, receive a reasoned decision within a published service standard, open a bank account on settled terms, hold customer assets on a legally perfected basis, and appeal an adverse decision to a forum that exists.

On 11 August 2026, none of those six things was reliably available. That, and not any judgement about virtual assets as a class, is the finding of this report.

Conclusion

C.1 The finding, restated once

Pakistan legislated before it built. The Virtual Assets Act, 2026 is a real statute of ordinary legislative quality, internationally intelligible in its architecture, and it is in force. What stands behind it, at the evidence cut-off of 11 August 2026, is not yet a functioning legal order: the licensing regulations were unauthenticated drafts; the Travel Rule had no threshold; the issuer pathway had no machinery; the appellate tribunal had no members and, on the qualification and age provisions read together, possibly no lawfully appointable presiding officer; the anti-money-laundering designation was incomplete; and the tax provision named a statute that does not exist while creating no charge. None of these findings depends on any view about virtual assets as an asset class. Each rests on the words of an instrument read against the words of another instrument, and each can be checked against the sources listed in Annex B.

C.2 Why the distinction matters

The report's controlling distinction — legislative success achieved, operational success untested (Chapter 1, §1.3) — is not rhetorical framing. It determines legal consequences. A provider that relies on a draft handbook as though it were a rule is not merely optimistic; it is uninsured against the argument, available to any future litigant or regulator, that the document it complied with never had legal effect. A ministry that announces a regulated market before the licence can be identified and obtained invites the same argument at sovereign scale, in front of assessors and treaty partners for whom the difference between a gazetted instrument and an announcement is precisely what is being assessed. The eight defects that only Parliament or a specifically authorised notification can cure (Chapter 9, items 1–8) do not become smaller with time. They compound: every licence issued, account opened and asset custodied on the present foundations inherits its uncertainties.

C.3 The window

The six-month transitional window under section 70 closes on 4 September 2026. The report has resisted treating that date as drama, and resists it here: it is a statutory computation, flagged for verification at Annex D, item D-1. But it is the one date on which the framework's two possible characters separate. If, by that date, the Horizon 1 actions at §23.2 have been taken — the corrective bill introduced, the licensing framework published or its absence honestly and legally accounted for, transitional directions issued, the supervisory designation completed — then the gap between statute and system is being closed in the only way it can be closed: by instruments. If they have not, the framework enters its operational phase governed by drafts, and every subsequent step is built on the deferral. The window is short, but nothing in Horizon 1 requires new policy: each action executes a decision the statute has already made.

C.4 What this report has tried to change

Beyond its findings, the report has argued for a method. Every substantive claim in it carries a status tag; every unverified proposition is flagged, not suppressed; internal working papers are cited as controlled documents, never as authority; and no aggregate reading claim is made anywhere in the text. The method is the message. A regulator that publishes a rule register (§23.2, item 1.7), keeps its instruments' status legible, and measures itself by proofs of completion rather than announcements will not need reports of this kind, because the questions this report exists to answer — what is law, what is draft, what is gap — will be answerable from the public record. That, more than any single amendment, is the reform.

C.5 Closing proposition

The question this report set out to answer was whether Pakistan's virtual-asset framework, as it stands, can bear the weight about to be placed on it. The answer is: not yet, and the deficit is specific, enumerated and curable. The corrective programme at Chapter 23 is sequenced by legal authority, dated, and paired with proofs of completion. What remains is execution — and execution, in a legal system, has only one currency: authenticated, published, commenced instruments. Everything else is commentary, including this report.

+Not a licence count. Not an announcement. 23.6 What would count as success

Not a licence count. Not an announcement. The measure is whether a competent applicant, on a defined date, can identify the licence, submit a complete application, receive a reasoned decision within a published service standard, open a bank account on settled terms, hold customer assets on a legally perfected basis, and appeal an adverse decision to a forum that exists.

On 11 August 2026, none of those six things was reliably available. That, and not any judgement about virtual assets as a class, is the finding of this report.
