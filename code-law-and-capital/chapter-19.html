19.1 The United Kingdom after Brexit

19.1.1 A distinct domestic legal order

1
The United Kingdom is no longer within the EU legislative system. Parliament, domestic regulators and UK courts determine its cryptoasset order, subject to retained or assimilated law and international obligations. The result is staged: existing financial-services categories, anti-money-laundering registration and financial-promotion rules operate now, while a wider statutory regime has been enacted for later commencement.1

19.1.2 Regulatory interaction with, but separation from, the European Union

2
UK firms may still encounter EU law by serving EU clients or establishing within a Member State. Equivalence, recognition or cooperation cannot be presumed. A UK permission does not constitute a MiCA authorisation, and an EU authorisation does not itself satisfy UK requirements. The two orders influence each other commercially but remain legally distinct.

19.2 UK Judicial Recognition

19.2.1 Cryptoassets as property

3
English courts treated cryptoassets as property capable of supporting proprietary relief before Parliament intervened. AA v Persons Unknown accepted Bitcoin as property for an interim proprietary injunction.2 The Property (Digital Assets etc) Act 2025 now confirms that a thing is not prevented from being an object of personal property rights merely because it is neither a thing in possession nor a thing in action.3 The Act removes a categorical barrier; it does not declare every digital record property or settle every incident of ownership.

19.2.2 Proprietary injunctions, tracing, service and disclosure

4
Courts have adapted freezing, proprietary and information remedies to pseudonymous transactions. Relief still depends on jurisdiction, a serious issue, evidence linking assets or defendants, and procedural fairness. Orders against exchanges can be effective because an identifiable intermediary holds records or controls withdrawals. They do not establish a general judicial power over every network participant.

19.3 UK Financial-Services Perimeter

19.3.1 Specified investments and regulated activities

5
Some token arrangements fall within the Financial Services and Markets Act 2000 because they embody specified investments or because an activity is regulated. Others sit outside that traditional perimeter. Classification therefore turns on rights and activity, not marketing terminology. The 2026 Cryptoassets Regulations establish the legal basis for a broader regime, but the Financial Conduct Authority states that the new regime will begin on 25 October 2027.4

19.3.2 Exchange, custody, financial promotions and registration

6
At the cut-off, cryptoasset businesses within scope must comply with Money Laundering Regulations registration, and promotions to UK consumers must comply with the financial-promotion regime even where the promoter is overseas.5 Registration is not a general prudential licence or endorsement. Firms should not describe the future 2027 regime as presently operative.

19.4 UK Payments, Stablecoins and Digital Securities

19.4.1 Payment services and stable-value activity

7
Stable-value arrangements may engage payment-services, electronic-money, banking or future cryptoasset rules depending on structure. The policy choice is not simply whether a token is “stable”; it is who owes redemption, what backs the claim, where reserves sit and what happens on failure. The comprehensive regime's later start makes transition and public communication especially important.

19.4.2 Digital securities, market infrastructure and tokenisation

8
The UK's Digital Securities Sandbox permits controlled modification of specified legislative requirements for eligible market-infrastructure activity. A sandbox is a bounded statutory environment, not a waiver of all law. Tokenisation can alter issuance and settlement processes while leaving the underlying security, issuer duties, market-abuse controls and finality questions intact.

19.5 UK Insolvency, Custody and Law Reform

19.5.1 Customer assets and intermediary failure

9
Property recognition is only the first step in failure. A customer must establish the nature of the entitlement, the governing terms, segregation or trust arrangements, and any shortfall allocation. Wallet labels and on-chain balances do not by themselves prove the intermediary's liabilities. Custody rules should therefore join legal characterisation to operational reconciliation and return procedures.

19.5.2 Law Commission and legislative development

10
The Law Commission's 2023 final report recommended targeted development rather than a closed statutory taxonomy.6 The 2025 Act implements the core recognition point while leaving courts to develop incidents case by case. That approach preserves common-law adaptability but requires litigation capacity and cannot replace clear sectoral rules for licensed custodians.

19.6 The European Union as a Supranational Legal Order

19.6.1 Institutional competence and directly applicable legislation

11
MiCA is an EU regulation and is directly applicable within Member States according to its terms.7 It allocates functions among national competent authorities, the European Banking Authority and the European Securities and Markets Authority. This legal form differs fundamentally from voluntary regional coordination.

19.6.2 Member-state implementation and residual national law

12
Member States designate authorities, exercise options and apply sanctions and procedure within the EU framework. National contract, property, insolvency, company and procedural law continues to matter where EU law does not fully harmonise the consequence. A MiCA authorisation therefore does not answer every ownership or insolvency question.

19.7 Markets in Crypto-Assets Regulation

19.7.1 Authorisation, issuers and service providers

13
MiCA creates disclosure and conduct rules for offers and admission to trading, and authorisation and organisational duties for cryptoasset service providers. It also creates a passport within the Union under specified conditions. The passport is possible because a supranational regulation and common institutional architecture supply the legal basis; it is not a generic benefit of licensing.

19.7.2 Asset-referenced and electronic-money tokens

14
MiCA distinguishes asset-referenced tokens from electronic-money tokens and imposes issuer, reserve, redemption, governance and supervisory requirements. Significance can trigger enhanced oversight. The categories show the value of separating the reference mechanism, issuer obligation and use in payment from market vocabulary.

19.8 Transfer of Funds and AML Framework

19.8.1 Travel Rule and information duties

15
Regulation (EU) 2023/1113 extends originator and beneficiary information requirements to transfers of cryptoassets involving covered providers.8 Compliance requires verified data, handling of missing information and risk-based decisions. It does not make every unhosted-wallet transfer unlawful; the applicable duties depend on regulated-provider involvement and the Regulation's conditions.

19.8.2 AML authority, supervision and sanctions

16
MiCA authorisation does not displace anti-money-laundering law. Customer due diligence, suspicious-transaction reporting, sanctions screening and record retention operate through the EU and Member-State AML architecture. Information sharing must retain legal purpose, confidentiality and redress.

19.9 DLT Market Infrastructure and Tokenised Finance

19.9.1 Pilot regime and market infrastructure

17
Regulation (EU) 2022/858 permits authorised DLT market infrastructures to operate within a supervised pilot subject to limits and possible exemptions.9 The pilot is evidence-generating legislation: it allows specified departures while preserving entry, reporting, investor-protection and exit controls.

19.9.2 Securities law, settlement and central-bank interaction

18
DLT financial instruments remain financial instruments. Settlement assets, finality, delivery-versus-payment and access to central-bank money remain institutional questions. A ledger cannot confer settlement finality unless the governing law gives the recorded event that consequence.

19.10 Prudential Treatment and Operational Resilience

19.10.1 Capital, custody and concentration

19
Capital, own-funds, safeguarding and insurance requirements respond to different loss channels. Outsourced custody can concentrate technology and legal risk even where each firm meets an individual requirement. Supervision should examine group structure, common service providers and the recoverability of customer records.

19.10.2 DORA, outsourcing and incident control

20
The Digital Operational Resilience Act applies a harmonised framework for ICT risk, incident reporting, resilience testing and critical third-party oversight to covered financial entities.10 Its contribution is organisational, not merely technical: boards retain responsibility when functions are outsourced.

19.11 Data Protection and Digital Identity

19.11.1 GDPR and cross-border data

21
The General Data Protection Regulation requires a lawful basis, purpose limitation, data minimisation, security and observance of transfer rules.11 Immutability does not excuse unlawful processing. System design should distinguish public transaction data from identity, compliance and supervisory records and avoid placing unnecessary personal data on a public ledger.

19.11.2 Identity, privacy and supervisory access

22
Digital identity can reduce duplicate verification and improve inclusion, but creates concentration and exclusion risks. Supervisory access must be authorised, necessary and logged; private firms should not receive undefined public powers. Privacy and financial integrity require structured access rather than a choice between anonymity and total visibility.

19.12 UK–EU Comparison and Lessons for Pakistan

19.12.1 Common-law development versus supranational legislation

23
The UK combines common-law recognition with staged financial legislation. The EU combines a directly applicable market code with residual national private law. Neither model eliminates classification, institutional capacity or transition. Their different constitutional bases explain why an EU passport and UK judicial development cannot be copied as isolated devices.

19.12.2 Transferable rules and institutional limits

24
Pakistan can adapt express issuer redemption duties, activity permissions, custody safeguards, operational-resilience governance, proportionate promotions rules and versioned transition. It should not assume EU-style supranational institutions, nor leave core customer-asset consequences wholly to litigation. Domestic constitutional competence and the notified PVARA baseline remain controlling.
25
The two orders reveal a common sequence beneath their different legal forms. A regulator first identifies the activity and responsible person; entry requirements then test governance, capital and systems; continuing rules govern conduct, custody and reporting; failure rules protect customers and preserve records; and courts or review bodies determine contested applications. Pakistan's Regulations address significant parts of entry and continuing conduct. Primary and institutional work remains necessary at the interfaces, especially property, insolvency, tax, banking and constitutional review.
26
Stablecoin regulation illustrates the sequence. MiCA legislates distinct issuer categories and supervisory consequences. The UK is moving toward a domestic regime through staged legislation and rules. Neither model permits the label “stablecoin” to answer reserve ownership, redemption, payment use or failure. Pakistan should require the responsible issuer to state the holder's enforceable claim, reserve assets and custodian, valuation, reconciliation, redemption time, suspension events and insolvency consequence before public distribution.
27
Custody supplies a second test. Property recognition under the 2025 UK Act makes it possible for a digital object to support personal property rights, but the Act does not prove that a particular platform holds the object for the customer. MiCA's custody and organisational rules improve regulated conduct but leave residual national private law. Pakistan therefore needs both: a technology-neutral recognition rule and prescriptive duties making customer entitlement reconstructable across omnibus wallets and sub-custody chains.
28
The comparison also warns against confusing regulatory scale with legal simplicity. MiCA's passport reduces repeated authorisation inside the Union because Member States share a regulation, institutions and judicial order. It also generates extensive technical standards and supervisory coordination. The UK's staged regime avoids claiming that future comprehensive rules already operate. Pakistan should use its single national framework to reduce duplication, but a licence should still list permitted activities and conditions rather than function as an unlimited badge.
29
Operational resilience should be owned at board level. DORA's most transferable insight is that outsourcing changes the delivery of a function, not the regulated firm's responsibility for it. A Pakistani provider using a cloud host, wallet technology vendor, analytics service or foreign custodian should map concentration, access, incident notification, termination and recovery. PVARA needs lawful access to sufficient evidence, while customer and personal data remain protected by purpose and security controls.
30
Data law creates a genuine tension with public ledgers. A firm cannot always delete or correct a public transaction record, yet it can avoid adding identity data, keep personal information off-chain, use revocable credentials and correct linked institutional records. Supervisory design should ask what information is necessary for licensing, AML, tax and redress and who may see it. An indiscriminate data lake would not be justified by either GDPR comparison or innovation policy.
31
Finally, both orders show the value of transition candour. The FCA publicly distinguishes current registration and promotion duties from the regime commencing in October 2027. EU instruments specify application dates and transitional arrangements. Pakistan's institutions should do the same for each form, permission and continuing service. A clear date and a bounded continuity rule protect lawful reliance more effectively than informal assurance.

32
Enforcement architecture completes the comparison. UK regulators act within domestic statutory powers and their decisions are subject to domestic review; EU implementation combines national competent authorities with EBA, ESMA and the Court of Justice structure. Administrative cooperation does not eliminate the affected firm's right to know the case and challenge a decision. Pakistan should specify which body investigates, which imposes an administrative measure, what material may be withheld, when interim action is available and which forum reviews the result.
33
Market-abuse and promotion rules also need to be separated. A misleading advertisement may be actionable even where the asset itself falls outside a securities category; manipulation rules depend on the market and instrument within scope. MiCA supplies express conduct rules for covered cryptoassets, while the UK applies its promotions regime broadly to communications to UK consumers. Pakistan should give PVARA clear authority over regulated-service promotions and coordinate with SECP where a tokenised security is involved, avoiding double punishment for the same conduct.
34
The independent contribution of the comparison is thus institutional rather than ideological. Common-law adaptability and supranational codification can both protect markets if source, competence and remedies are visible. Each can also fail when customers cannot establish entitlement or regulators cannot supervise outsourced infrastructure. Pakistan's design should be judged by those operational questions rather than whether its statute resembles London or Brussels.
35
That judgment should be published periodically. A concise implementation report can state permissions granted, processing time, customer-asset exceptions, material incidents, complaints, appeals and supervisory limitations. Comparable evidence would allow legislators to decide whether primary law, Regulations or institutional resources need adjustment, and would keep formal legal convergence from concealing weak execution.
36
Pakistan should also preserve a public perimeter archive. As in the UK's staged change and the EU's application of successive instruments, a transaction must be tested against the law then in force. The archive should show effective dates, transitional permissions, superseded guidance and the status of each provider. This protects both enforcement and legitimate reliance and prevents later policy from being projected backwards.

Chapter 19 Conclusion and Standalone Contribution

37
The UK and EU demonstrate two credible routes toward a digital-asset order, but neither is a single template. Public authorisation, private rights, AML controls, resilience and data law must meet at operational interfaces. Pakistan's task is to allocate those interfaces expressly and to state when a new permission becomes legally and practically available.

Chapter 19 Selected Authorities and Independent-Use Note

38
This chapter stands as a comparative account of UK and EU law at the cut-off. UK readers should note the scheduled 25 October 2027 commencement of the comprehensive regime; later rules or commencement changes require updating.
