Chapter 21 — Publication Candidate

Contents

Chapter Summary

1
[21.01] This chapter examines the GCC setting, the United Arab Emirates’ federal order, Dubai’s Virtual Assets Regulatory Authority, the ADGM and DIFC financial free zones, and the approaches of Bahrain, Saudi Arabia, Qatar, Oman and Kuwait. It also considers Islamic-finance governance and cross-border capital. Each permission is analysed by territory, issuer, activity and regulator. The chapter rejects the assumption that one licence supplies access across a federation or region.

21.1 The GCC as a Regional Setting

21.1.1 Institutional cooperation without a uniform digital-asset code

2
[21.02] The Gulf Cooperation Council supports coordination, integration and connection between Member States, including in economic and financial affairs.1 It has not, however, created a directly applicable digital-asset code comparable to the European Union’s MiCA regime. Licensing, monetary policy, securities law and enforcement remain national or, where lawfully established, free-zone functions. Regional similarity should not be converted into a fictional passport.

21.1.2 Member-state sovereignty and market competition

3
[21.03] Member States compete for capital through different regulators, free zones, tax settings and innovation programmes. Competition can accelerate rulemaking and expertise, but it can also produce overlapping claims. Comparative analysis must identify the territory, excluded zones, regulated activity and legal status of every instrument.

21.2 United Arab Emirates: Federal Order

21.2.1 Federal virtual-asset and financial regulation

4
[21.04] Federal Cabinet Decisions Nos 111 and 112 of 2022 established a framework for virtual assets and their service providers outside financial free zones and delegated specified federal competencies to Dubai’s Virtual Assets Regulatory Authority.2 The framework interacts with emirate-level and free-zone laws. A provider must therefore map where it operates and which activity it performs rather than rely on a generic ‘UAE licence’.

21.2.2 Central Bank and Capital Market Authority

5
[21.05] The Central Bank of the UAE regulates payment-token services within its monetary and banking remit. Its Payment Token Services Regulation defines the territorial and activity scope, prohibits unlicensed payment-token services and establishes licence categories.3 Federal Decree-Law No 32 of 2025 established the Capital Market Authority as the legal successor to the Securities and Commodities Authority; references in legislation to the former authority are now read accordingly.4 Token form does not displace the underlying regulator’s competence.

21.3 Dubai and VARA

21.3.1 Territorial and activity-based regulation

6
[21.06] Dubai Law No 4 of 2022 created the Virtual Assets Regulatory Authority. VARA’s territorial remit covers Dubai, including development and free zones, but excludes the Dubai International Financial Centre.5 The exclusion is constitutionally and commercially important: the DIFC has its own regulator and courts.

21.3.2 Modular rulebooks, licensing and enforcement

7
[21.07] VARA combines compulsory company-wide rulebooks with activity-specific modules for advisory, broker-dealer, custody, exchange, lending and borrowing, management and investment, transfer and settlement, and issuance.6 This makes obligations traceable to functions. Its value nevertheless depends on supervision, inspections, enforcement and orderly exit, not the modular presentation alone.

21.4 ADGM and DIFC

21.4.1 Financial free-zone legal orders

8
[21.08] The Abu Dhabi Global Market and Dubai International Financial Centre are distinct financial free-zone legal orders with their own regulators and courts under UAE constitutional and federal arrangements. ADGM’s Financial Services Regulatory Authority has developed and updated a virtual-asset framework; the Dubai Financial Services Authority regulates crypto-token financial services in the DIFC under rules updated with effect from 12 January 2026.7

21.4.2 Courts, regulators, common-law frameworks and jurisdictional overlap

9
[21.09] Common-law courts and specialised regulators can improve commercial predictability, but only within their jurisdiction. A free-zone entity may still encounter onshore marketing, payment, employment, insolvency or enforcement rules. Interfaces need formal memoranda, referral rules and judicial conflict methods; a corporate address cannot settle them.

21.5 Bahrain

21.5.1 Central-bank licensing and regulated cryptoasset services

10
[21.10] The Central Bank of Bahrain requires a licence for regulated cryptoasset services and specifies the relevant service categories and licence classes.8 Centralising monetary and market-supervision functions can reduce institutional ambiguity, but also demands sufficient specialist capacity and transparent due process.

21.5.2 Prudential, conduct and financial-integrity controls

11
[21.11] Bahrain’s framework combines governance and conduct controls with segregation, cyber-security, anti-money-laundering and reporting obligations. Its 2025 stablecoin module requires a licence and, for the covered single-currency stablecoins, full reserve backing and redemption controls.9 A stablecoin permission answers only the specified issuance and service activities; it does not immunise securities, payments or consumer conduct.

21.6 Saudi Arabia

21.6.1 Monetary and securities policy

12
[21.12] In 2018 the Saudi Standing Committee warned that virtual currencies were not official currencies and that no parties were then licensed to trade them in the Kingdom.10 That dated warning should not be converted into a timeless general prohibition. The current legal question remains activity-specific: banking, payment, securities and promotional conduct each require authority under the competent regime.

21.6.2 Fintech development, tokenisation and regulatory caution

13
[21.13] The Saudi Central Bank’s regulatory sandbox and the Capital Market Authority’s Financial Technology Experimental Permit provide controlled routes for testing eligible innovations.11 These routes separate experimentation from market-wide authorisation. Pakistan can adopt bounded testing only if entry, affected rules, customer exposure, data access and exit consequences are published and legally controlled.

21.7 Qatar, Oman and Kuwait

21.7.1 Distinct national approaches

14
[21.14] The Qatar Financial Centre introduced a Digital Assets Framework in 2024 for permitted tokens and token-service activities within the QFC. Its rules expressly exclude tokens used as currency substitutes or means of payment, including cryptocurrencies and stablecoins, from the permitted-token category.12 The framework is therefore a controlled tokenisation regime, not a general cryptoasset permission.
15
[21.15] Oman’s Financial Services Authority has issued instructions for registering virtual-asset service providers and applying anti-money-laundering and counter-terrorist-financing requirements, while continuing work on a broader framework.13 Kuwait’s Capital Markets Authority Circular No 10 of 2023 prohibits specified uses, investments, licensing and mining activities within its stated remit and links contravention to the penalties in the Anti-Money Laundering and Combating the Financing of Terrorism Law.14

21.7.2 Restriction, experimentation and institutional development

16
[21.16] These approaches cannot be collapsed into a regional average. Registration for financial integrity is not a market licence; a financial-centre framework is not necessarily nationwide permission; and a prohibition must be read for scope, authority and exceptions. The disciplined comparator is the legal effect of each instrument.

21.8 Islamic Finance and Digital Assets

21.8.1 Shariah characterisation and governance

17
[21.17] There is no single universally controlling Shariah characterisation of every digital asset. Analysis may differ by asset, jurisdiction, institutional board and contractual structure. A lawful product-governance process should identify the asset and promises, obtain reasoned Shariah review where represented as compliant, manage conflicts and monitor continuing conformity. The Islamic Financial Services Board’s 2025 principles emphasise the institutional allocation of responsibility, procedures, conflicts controls, review and audit rather than a universal product label.15

21.8.2 Asset backing, uncertainty, speculation and product design

18
[21.18] Asset backing does not by itself establish compliance, and volatility does not by itself decide it. Product design should examine riba, excessive gharar, maysir, sale of what is not owned, custody, redemption, risk allocation and connection to the real economy. Claims of compliance must be specific, documented and institutionally accountable.

21.9 Cross-Border Capital and Regional Competition

21.9.1 Free zones, investment and market access

19
[21.19] Free zones can concentrate courts, regulators, professional services and international capital. Their effectiveness comes from a complete institutional package, not reduced friction alone. Market access outside the zone, banking connectivity and recognition of judgments remain separate questions.

21.9.2 Regulatory arbitrage and supervisory cooperation

20
[21.20] Firms may allocate issuance, custody, management and marketing across different entities. Supervisors therefore need group maps, beneficial ownership, consolidated risk information and lawful exchange arrangements. Cooperation should preserve confidentiality and contestability; it should not create powers that domestic law has not conferred.

21.10 Lessons and Limits for Pakistan

21.10.1 Modularity, speed and institutional capability

21
[21.21] Modular rulebooks, precise activity permissions, public registers, specialist supervision and defined interfaces are transferable techniques. Pakistan’s final Virtual Asset Services Regulations and Activity Specific Regulations were notified on 21 August 2026 under SROs 1419(I)/2026 and 1420(I)/2026.16 Speed is valuable only when valid instruments are supported by staff, systems, supervision and courts.
22
[21.22] The general regulations state their hierarchy, territorial reach and proportional licensing approach. They also require PVARA to maintain a public register of licensed providers.17 That register should identify the legal entity, regulator, activity, customer class, conditions, effective date and current status of each permission.
23
[21.23] Modularity allows a common governance base to be joined to activity risk. A custodian needs key control, segregation and recovery; an exchange needs order handling, surveillance and conflicts management; an issuer needs disclosure, reserves and redemption; and a transfer service needs execution and financial-integrity controls. Modules should be cumulative where one group performs several activities. Group structure should not permit interconnected functions to be distributed among affiliates to avoid combined-risk supervision.

21.10.2 Constitutional and judicial limits

24
[21.24] Pakistan cannot reproduce an emirate/free-zone architecture without constitutional authority. Regulatory design must respect federal and provincial legislative competence, equality and property protections, the constitutional allocation of judicial power and High Court review.18 Branding an enclave as innovative cannot cure an unlawful delegation.
25
[21.25] The Virtual Assets Act 2026 establishes a Virtual Assets Appellate Tribunal and provides a further appeal to the Supreme Court.19 A specialist tribunal can improve first-instance expertise only if appointments, procedure, independence, reasoned decisions and appeals are operational. Its statutory jurisdiction must coexist with, and cannot displace, the constitutional jurisdiction of the superior courts.

21.10.3 Territorial scope, modular permissions and cross-border recognition

26
[21.26] The UAE comparison shows why geographic scope belongs on every public register. A provider may be licensed for a particular activity by VARA in Dubai outside the DIFC, by the DFSA in the DIFC, by the FSRA in ADGM or under a federal framework. Customers encountering a common national brand may not understand those distinctions.
27
[21.27] A Gulf licence may make a provider commercially credible but does not answer whether it may solicit Pakistani customers, hold their assets abroad or transfer their data. Pakistan’s Act and regulations address territorial and extraterritorial reach and regulatory cooperation; recognition should still be activity-specific and conditional on lawful cooperation, equivalent outcomes, access to records, complaint handling and orderly withdrawal.20 Automatic passporting would outsource public protection without a treaty or common legal order.

29
[21.28] Saudi Arabia, Qatar, Oman and Kuwait demonstrate that regulatory development can be selective. A state may authorise controlled testing, permit tokenisation within a financial centre, require AML registration, or prohibit specified activities while studying broader policy. A limited rule can be legitimate if its scope and rationale are clear. The danger lies in allowing pilots, warnings or registrations to carry legal meanings they do not possess.
30
[21.29] Institutional speed should be measured from valid decision to operational evidence, not press release to announcement. Useful evidence includes processed applications, examination findings, tested incident contacts, successful customer-asset reconciliation and reasoned enforcement or appeal outcomes. Pakistan’s sequencing should make the dependency between law, staffing and market infrastructure explicit.

21.10.4 Failure, public finance and Shariah governance

31
[21.30] Failure planning is a necessary comparator. A provider may custody assets through an affiliate or foreign sub-custodian and serve customers elsewhere. Its insolvency can engage several courts and regulators despite a specialised home regime. Pakistan’s final regulations require custody controls, proof of reserves and insolvency-related arrangements.21 Providers should also maintain a custody-chain map, governing-law disclosure, portable records and an exercised transfer or return plan.
32
[21.31] Public finance deserves equal care. PVARA’s Fund is a statutory, lapsable fund subject to the Public Finance Management Act 2019. Constitutional custody, appropriation and audit controls continue to apply.22 A projected licence market should not justify commitments before lawful revenue and appropriation exist. Independence is strengthened by transparent funding and accountability, not by placing funds beyond ordinary control.
33
[21.32] Shariah governance may support regional cooperation, but any mutual-recognition scheme would need competent approval, defined standards, conflicts controls and the ability to revisit a product after its structure changes. The safer immediate approach is disclosure of the reviewing body, reasoning basis and continuing status so investors understand precisely what has and has not been certified. The financial regulator remains responsible for solvency, custody, disclosure and market conduct.

21.10.5 Technology, competition and transparency

34
[21.33] Technology procurement is another capacity test. A regulator or provider may purchase sophisticated market-surveillance, custody and compliance systems, but public authority remains with the regulator. Pakistan should specify data ownership, access, model limitations, security, continuity, audit and exit before outsourcing. A vendor’s risk score should lead to review, not become an unpublished rule for licensing, account closure or enforcement.
35
[21.34] Competition policy should examine vertical integration. A group that issues a token, operates an exchange, provides custody, lends against the asset and controls a settlement token can concentrate conflicts and failure risk even where each module is individually licensed. PVARA should map the group, impose proportionate separation or governance controls and coordinate with the Competition Commission of Pakistan where dominance, restrictive agreements, deceptive marketing or merger questions arise.23
36
[21.35] Regional capital competition should not displace domestic public outcomes. Attraction of firms, assets under custody and transaction volume can rise while consumer recourse, market concentration or illicit-finance risk worsens. Pakistan should evaluate access, integrity, resilience, tax compliance and rights alongside investment. A competitive regime is one that produces reliable lawful service, not the lowest entry threshold.
37
[21.36] The final lesson is transparency about limits. An authority should say where its territory ends, where another regulator decides and where no permission yet exists. That candour reduces disputes and false reliance. Pakistan’s public positioning should make legal scope, customer protection, review and failure preparedness as visible as innovation opportunities.

Chapter 21 Conclusion

38
[21.37] The GCC demonstrates that regulatory competition can coexist with disciplined licensing and specialised courts, but also that territorial boundaries matter. Pakistan’s best lesson is to make every permission legible by actor, activity, territory and regulator, and to join Islamic-finance claims to reasoned product governance rather than general labels.
39
[21.38] This chapter is an independent jurisdictional map, not a licensing opinion. Firms must verify the current rulebook, geographic scope and activity permission with the relevant official register.
