20.1 Asia Is Not a Single Regulatory Model

20.1.1 Different constitutional, monetary and market structures

1
Japan and South Korea regulate exchanges within detailed national frameworks; Singapore and Hong Kong use financial-centre institutions; China restricts private virtual-currency business while developing state digital money; India combines tax and AML obligations with unresolved perimeter questions; Australia is transitioning to new platform licensing; New Zealand relies substantially on existing law. These differences follow constitutional authority, capital controls, monetary policy and market history.

20.1.2 Licensing, restriction, experimentation and state digital currency

2
Licensing, prohibition, sandbox experimentation and central-bank digital currency are different legal techniques. A state may permit wholesale tokenisation while restricting retail promotion; it may tax a transaction without licensing the service; and it may develop a CBDC without recognising private tokens as money. Comparison must keep those propositions separate.

20.2 Japan

20.2.1 Mt Gox, judicial characterisation and insolvency

3
The Mt Gox failure made custody, insolvency and record integrity central to Japanese policy. Early judicial reasoning did not treat Bitcoin as a corporeal thing capable of ownership under the particular Civil Code inquiry, but this did not place exchange claims outside law. Claims, restitution and insolvency distributions continued through contractual and statutory structures. The episode warns against deriving an entire regulatory perimeter from one property-law characterisation.

20.2.2 Exchange licensing, custody, customer assets and stablecoins

4
The Payment Services Act requires registration for cryptoasset exchange services and imposes information, security, segregation and supervisory duties. Stablecoin reforms distinguish electronic payment instruments and limit issuance or intermediation through designated regulated actors. Japan also applies FATF-style transfer-information duties; an August 2026 amendment expanded covered jurisdictions.1 Precise commencement and counterparty scope remain essential.

20.3 Singapore

20.3.1 Payment-services regulation and licensing

5
Singapore regulates digital payment token services through the Payment Services Act 2019, Monetary Authority of Singapore notices and amendments extending the perimeter to specified cross-border and custody activities. A licence is activity-specific. AML, technology risk, safeguarding and conduct requirements may differ by service and customer class.2

20.3.2 Automated transactions, wholesale tokenisation and institutional markets

6
Singapore's Electronic Transactions Act supports automated contracting subject to ordinary requirements of attribution and validity. Project Guardian and related institutional pilots explore tokenised funds and wholesale markets. A pilot or industry standard is not general permission: regulated issuance, dealing, custody and settlement continue to depend on applicable legislation and MAS authority.

20.4 Hong Kong

20.4.1 Virtual-asset trading platforms and securities law

7
Centralised virtual-asset trading platforms operating in Hong Kong are subject to the Anti-Money Laundering and Counter-Terrorist Financing Ordinance licensing regime; platforms dealing in security tokens may also require authorisation under the Securities and Futures Ordinance. The Securities and Futures Commission applies a dual-licensing approach and detailed platform guidelines.3

20.4.2 Institutional access, tokenisation and stablecoin development

8
Hong Kong combines licensed retail platform access with institutional tokenisation and a distinct fiat-referenced stablecoin regime. The Stablecoins Ordinance commenced on 1 August 2025, and the Hong Kong Monetary Authority announced its first licences in April 2026.4 Licensing an issuer does not settle the treatment of every tokenised security, platform or custody arrangement.

20.5 People’s Republic of China

20.5.1 Restrictions on private cryptoasset activity

9
The 2021 multi-agency notice treats virtual-currency-related business activities as illegal financial activities and addresses overseas exchanges serving domestic residents.5 This is a restrictions model, not merely an unlicensed-market model. Any account must preserve the distinction between private holding, prohibited business services and enforcement practice rather than state that “crypto is banned” without qualification.

20.5.2 Blockchain policy, state digital currency and monetary control

10
China's support for blockchain applications and its digital-renminbi programme coexist with restrictions on privately issued and traded virtual currencies. The apparent tension disappears once the state distinguishes infrastructure from private monetary substitution. For Pakistan, the lesson is that technology policy, payment sovereignty and private-asset regulation are separable decisions.

20.6 South Korea

20.6.1 Investor protection, exchanges and market conduct

11
The Act on the Protection of Virtual Asset Users took effect on 19 July 2024. It imposes customer-deposit and custody safeguards, requires a high proportion of customer virtual assets to be kept in cold wallets, and addresses unfair trading and supervisory enforcement.6 The statute is a protection layer; it does not by itself settle every issuer or business category.

20.6.2 Tax, custody and institutional development

12
South Korea continues to develop its second-stage market architecture. August 2026 amendments strengthened registration and AML controls for virtual-asset service providers, with provisions commencing according to the official instrument.7 Tax commencement has been politically contested; comparative claims should cite the current tax statute and effective date rather than repeated announcements.

20.7 India

20.7.1 Banking restrictions and constitutional litigation

13
In Internet and Mobile Association of India v Reserve Bank of India, the Supreme Court set aside the RBI's 2018 banking circular on proportionality grounds.8 The judgment did not declare virtual assets legal tender, create a licence, or prevent Parliament and regulators from acting within lawful authority. It is principally a case about evidential justification and the proportionality of a particular restriction.

20.7.2 Taxation, enforcement, regulatory uncertainty and recognition questions

14
India taxes income from transfers of virtual digital assets under section 115BBH and requires covered providers to comply with anti-money-laundering registration and guidance.9 Tax and AML coverage do not amount to comprehensive market authorisation. Private-law characterisation, service permissions and the treatment of decentralised arrangements remain questions requiring the relevant statute and facts.

20.8 Australia

20.8.1 Financial-services and market-law perimeter

15
ASIC applies existing corporations, financial-products, consumer and market law according to the rights and activities involved. Some cryptoassets are financial products; others are not. Misleading conduct and managed-investment-scheme rules can apply without a special token label.10

20.8.2 Licensing reform, custody and consumer protection

16
The Corporations Amendment (Digital Assets Framework) Act 2026 received Royal Assent on 8 April 2026 and introduces a licensing framework for covered digital-asset platforms and tokenised custody platforms. ASIC states that the new requirements commence on 9 April 2027 and has set an implementation path.11 Until commencement, present law continues; passage should not be described as present operational authorisation.

20.9 New Zealand

20.9.1 Ruscoe v Cryptopia and property in insolvency

17
In Ruscoe v Cryptopia Ltd (in liquidation), the High Court held that cryptocurrencies were property under the Companies Act 1993 and that the exchange held specified assets on trust for account holders.12 The holding depended on New Zealand law and the platform arrangements. It is persuasive comparative authority, not a universal declaration of customer priority.

20.9.2 Regulatory treatment and service-provider obligations

18
New Zealand applies financial-markets, fair-trading, AML and financial-service-provider law according to activity. The Financial Markets Authority has warned that particular offers or services may be regulated even without a bespoke cryptoasset code. Cryptopia shows why regulatory coverage should be joined to clear custody records and insolvency rules.

20.10 Comparative Lessons for Pakistan

20.10.1 Recognition, licensing, custody and transition

19
Across the region, the durable elements are express entry controls, segregation and reconciliation, market-conduct prohibitions, operational resilience and lawful transition. Property recognition and market authorisation answer different questions. Pakistan's notified PVARA Regulations provide a current licensing baseline; implementation should state how existing providers enter it and how customer assets are treated during transition and failure.

20.10.2 What diverse Asian models reveal about institutional fit

20
Financial-centre models rely on regulators, courts, payment systems and professional capacity that took time to build. Restriction models reflect different monetary and political choices. Pakistan should adapt mechanisms only after identifying the domestic institution, statute, resources and route of review that will make them real.
21
The first regional lesson concerns institutional memory after failure. Mt Gox and Cryptopia made the legal status of customer assets and the quality of platform records unavoidable. South Korea translated loss experience into statutory deposit and cold-wallet protections; Japan imposed segregation and exchange supervision. The transferable rule is not a fixed cold-storage percentage. It is the requirement that a supervisor and insolvency office-holder can reconstruct each customer's entitlement, identify control, detect a shortfall and activate a lawful return or distribution process.
22
The second lesson is that payment policy can diverge from investment-market policy. China supports distributed technology and a state digital currency while restricting private virtual-currency business. Indonesia permits supervised cryptoasset trading while maintaining a separate payment perimeter. Hong Kong regulates trading platforms and stablecoin issuers through different statutes and institutions. Pakistan should therefore resist using PVARA licensing to imply that an asset is legal tender, an authorised payment instrument or freely convertible under foreign-exchange law.
23
The third lesson is staged statutory commencement. Australia enacted a new framework in April 2026 but set commencement for April 2027. That interval allows rules, applications and systems to be prepared while existing law continues. Hong Kong's stablecoin law tied issuer entry to a commenced statutory regime and official licensing. Pakistan's regulations are already the current baseline; its next transition task is different. It must publish how an incumbent completes the application, which existing services may continue, what marketing is prohibited and what occurs on refusal.
24
The fourth lesson is that constitutional adjudication can improve regulatory method without deciding substantive policy. The Indian Supreme Court's proportionality ruling addressed the evidential foundation and breadth of a banking restriction. It did not confer monetary or licensing status. Pakistani regulators should similarly assemble evidence, identify affected rights, compare less restrictive means and give reasons, particularly where account access, blocking or compulsory exit is proposed.
25
The fifth lesson is to distinguish institutional experiments from general law. Singapore's wholesale tokenisation projects can test issuance, settlement and interoperability in a sophisticated market. They do not prove that retail distribution, custody or cross-border offers are generally permitted. A Pakistani sandbox should specify participants, modified or applicable rules, customer limits, data access, test metrics, exit and the legal effect of results. Graduation should require an ordinary permission or legislative change.

26
These comparisons also counsel against rankings. A restrictive jurisdiction may have a clear monetary policy but limited private innovation; a permissive financial centre may impose demanding entry and customer controls; a country taxing gains may still lack a complete service-provider regime. “Adoption” and “friendliness” are poor legal metrics. The questions that matter are whether citizens can know the law, firms can obtain a definite decision, assets survive failure, misconduct is investigated and restrictions can be challenged.
27
For Pakistan, institutional fit should be evidenced through capacity before scale. PVARA needs licence examiners, custody and technology specialists, a supervisory dataset and an exercised incident process. SBP and SECP need a recorded interface for mixed products. FBR needs valuation and reporting rules. Courts and investigators need admissible records. Comparative legislation becomes useful only when these domestic connections are made.
28
The licensing record should show more than an entity name. Japan, Hong Kong and South Korea demonstrate the importance of linking a provider to its activities and continuing duties. Pakistan's public register should list exchange, brokerage, custody, transfer, lending, issuance or other permissions separately, identify conditions and show suspension, surrender or wind-down. A customer should be able to distinguish a preliminary incorporation approval from a final activity licence without reading a press release.
29
Regional experience also supports risk-based foreign recognition rather than automatic equivalence. A provider licensed in Singapore, Hong Kong or Australia may be well supervised, but Pakistani authorities still need access to records, cooperation, customer remedies and a lawful route when permission changes abroad. Recognition should specify the activity and can be withdrawn on stated grounds. It should never imply that foreign monetary, tax or insolvency law displaces Pakistan's mandatory rules.
30
Finally, comparison should remain dynamic but source-controlled. Asia-Pacific regimes changed rapidly between 2024 and 2026. An annual review should record enactment, commencement, official rule changes, licensing evidence and leading judgments in separate columns. Policy speeches and consultation papers can explain direction but should not overwrite the current-law column. This discipline is particularly important where reform dates have been deferred or a pilot is described as a national framework.
31
Market integrity requires the same functional method. South Korea's unfair-trading controls, Hong Kong's platform supervision and Japan's exchange rules operate within distinct statutes. Pakistan should define manipulation, deceptive conduct, conflicts and misuse of customer orders for the markets it actually supervises. Surveillance data should support investigation, not automatically determine liability. Where SECP securities law and PVARA rules both engage the same conduct, referral and penalty coordination should preserve lawful competence and prevent duplicate punishment.
32
Retail access should be evaluated through evidence rather than foreign fashion. Suitability, warnings, leverage restrictions and product limits can reduce harm, but excessive complexity or broad banking exclusion can shift citizens to offshore channels. Pakistan should monitor complaints, losses, access, concentration and unauthorised-provider use, and revise controls through published reasons. That balanced feedback loop is more transferable than any region's present list of permitted assets.
33
Courts remain part of that loop. IAMAI and Cryptopia answered different constitutional and private-law questions, yet both show the value of reasoned adjudication when regulation or failure exposes an unresolved assumption. Pakistan should ensure that PVARA decisions generate a record fit for review and that insolvency proceedings can obtain custody evidence. Comparative doctrine is most useful when it improves that domestic decisional method.

Chapter 20 Conclusion

34
Asia-Pacific comparison defeats simple categories. Permission may coexist with strict retail controls; state tokenisation may coexist with restrictions on private currencies; and tax or AML coverage may coexist with an incomplete market perimeter. The correct comparative question is not which jurisdiction is friendliest, but which institution performs each legal function and with what evidence of operational capacity.
