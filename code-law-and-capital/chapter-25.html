25.1 The Cross-Border Character of Digital Assets

25.1.1 Distributed systems, intermediaries and users

1
A single transaction may involve an issuer in one state, protocol contributors in several, a custodian and sub-custodian elsewhere, a user in Pakistan and validator infrastructure worldwide. These facts create potential connections, not an answer. The relevant relationship may be contractual, proprietary, regulatory, tortious or insolvency-related.

25.1.2 Why location cannot be assumed from technology alone

2
A ledger copy exists in many places and a private key can move without changing the underlying claim. Server, node, key, owner and exchange locations can point in different directions. Any situs rule should be selected for a legal purpose and protect ascertainable third-party expectations.

25.2 Jurisdiction and Forum

25.2.1 Defendant, conduct, harm and contractual links

3
Jurisdiction may rest on presence, residence, submission, contractual forum, conduct or legally relevant harm, subject to domestic procedure. For unknown defendants, evidence may initially identify only addresses and transactions. Courts should require a real connection and useful relief rather than infer jurisdiction from worldwide accessibility.

25.2.2 Exclusive clauses, consumers and parallel proceedings

4
Platform terms often select a foreign forum. Courts should examine incorporation, notice, bargaining context, consumer protections and overriding statutes before enforcing the clause. Anti-suit, stay and parallel-proceeding rules must preserve comity and access to justice; boilerplate should not defeat mandatory protection by design.

25.3 Choice of Law

25.3.1 Express choice and objective connection

5
An express choice can govern contractual obligations if valid and sufficiently identified. It cannot necessarily determine proprietary effects against strangers. Without a choice, courts use the objectively closest or most real connection under the forum's method, considering performance, residence, intermediary and transaction structure.

25.3.2 Mandatory rules and public policy

6
Licensing, sanctions, consumer, AML, insolvency and market-integrity rules may apply irrespective of contractual choice. Public policy is an exceptional control, not a licence to disregard foreign law whenever outcomes differ. Courts should state which mandatory interest overrides and why.

25.4 Situs of a Digital Asset

25.4.1 Competing connecting factors

7
Proposed connecting factors include the owner's residence, location of the key, issuer, system, relevant intermediary or chosen law. Each works better for some assets than others. Owner residence may be unstable; key location may be secret or replicated; a permissionless network may have no issuer or administration.

25.4.2 Control, owner, intermediary, system and governing law

8
Legislation should use a hierarchy. For an intermediated holding, the law expressly governing the account or custody relationship can determine proprietary effects if third parties can ascertain it and protected persons are not prejudiced. For an issuer-based asset, issuer or system law may be appropriate. Control is a factual and legal concept, not a geographic point.

25.5 Proprietary and Contractual Characterisation

25.5.1 Rights against the world and personal obligations

9
A platform customer's claim may be a proprietary interest in identified assets, a beneficial interest in a pool, or a personal claim for delivery. The distinction governs priority, tracing and insolvency. Courts in Ruscoe v Cryptopia and ByBit Fintech Ltd v Ho Kai Xin recognised property and trust consequences on their particular laws and facts.1

25.5.2 Transfer, priority and remedies

10
The law governing proprietary effects should determine whether transfer occurs, whether a good-faith acquirer is protected and how security interests rank. The law governing a contract determines promises and damages. Remedies may be governed by the forum while depending on foreign-law rights; the categories should not be collapsed.

25.6 Tokens, Networks and Governing Law

25.6.1 Issuer terms, protocol rules and system governance

11
Issuer terms can choose law for issuer obligations and define redemption. Protocol rules describe technical validity but may not constitute an agreed choice among all holders. A governance vote can alter software; it cannot automatically amend a contract or extinguish property rights governed by law.

25.6.2 Changes, forks and absent central counterparties

12
Forks can create competing records, assets or communities. The legal consequence depends on exchange terms, custody duties, issuer promises and applicable property law. Where no central counterparty exists, courts should identify persons who made representations or exercised control rather than attribute obligations to a network label.

25.7 Custody and Intermediated Holdings

25.7.1 Account-based and wallet-based relationships

13
Omnibus accounts, individually segregated addresses, hosted wallets and non-custodial software allocate control differently. Contract, trust and regulatory status must be read together. A hosted-wallet balance can be an internal ledger claim even if the provider controls pooled on-chain assets.

25.7.2 Sub-custody, segregation and third-party effects

14
Cross-border sub-custody raises governing-law and insolvency questions at each tier. The primary custodian should disclose location and law, obtain records, reconcile holdings and preserve return rights. Contractual segregation that is not effective under the sub-custodian's insolvency law can provide false comfort.

25.8 Consumers, Investors and Decentralised Transactions

25.8.1 Protective jurisdiction and applicable law

15
Consumer rules may protect residents despite foreign terms where a business directs activity toward them. Investor-protection rules may apply to domestic offers or market conduct. The test should examine language, currency, marketing, customer acceptance and service design rather than mere website availability.

25.8.2 Front ends, protocols and actual control

16
An interface operator may select assets, route orders, charge fees or block users even where settlement occurs through a protocol. Those functions may create contractual, tortious or regulatory responsibility. Software publication alone presents different facts. “Decentralised” is a conclusion to prove, not a conflicts rule.

25.9 Recognition and Enforcement of Judgments

25.9.1 Foreign judgments and public policy

17
In Pakistan, sections 13 and 14 of the Code of Civil Procedure 1908 govern the conclusiveness and presumption concerning foreign judgments, subject to statutory exceptions including want of jurisdiction, absence of merits, natural justice, fraud and breach of Pakistani law.2 Reciprocity and execution procedure must be distinguished from recognition at common law.

25.9.2 Orders against exchanges, wallets and unknown defendants

18
English cases have authorised service and information relief against persons unknown and exchanges where procedural requirements were met.3 Recognition abroad depends on the requested state's rules and the nature of the order. A worldwide freezing label does not make the order self-executing in every country.

25.10 Cross-Border Insolvency

25.10.1 Centre of main interests and recognition

19
Cross-border insolvency requires identification of the debtor, proceeding and applicable recognition mechanism. The UNCITRAL Model Law offers a centre-of-main-interests architecture, but it operates only where enacted.4 Pakistan should not be described as having adopted it without current statutory confirmation.

25.10.2 Customer assets, tracing and cooperation

20
Recognition of a foreign proceeding does not determine whether customer assets belong to the debtor. Governing property and trust law, records and tracing remain relevant. Courts and office-holders need protocols for data, realisation and distribution while protecting local claimants and due process.

25.11 Freezing, Disclosure and Service-Provider Failure

25.11.1 Interim relief and information orders

21
Interim orders can prevent dissipation and identify defendants, but require urgency, evidence, undertakings and fair return dates. LMN v Bitflyer Holdings Inc illustrates disclosure against exchanges to trace cryptoassets.5 The order's effectiveness depended on identifiable intermediaries and cooperation.

25.11.2 Exchange, custodian and protocol failure

22
Failure can involve insolvency, exploit, fraud, governance breakdown or loss of keys. Each activates different law. Supervisors and courts should preserve records and customer assets before attempting a technological cure. Recovery claims should distinguish an asset, substitute proceeds and a personal claim.

25.12 Arbitration, Smart Contracts and DAOs

25.12.1 Agreement, seat and applicable law

23
Arbitration requires an agreement binding the parties, a seat and an arbitrable dispute. Code interaction is not necessarily consent. DAO governance terms may form an agreement for some participants if validly incorporated and accepted, but cannot bind strangers merely because they used a public network.

25.12.2 Awards, code-based disputes and enforcement

24
Pakistan's Recognition and Enforcement (Arbitration Agreements and Foreign Arbitral Awards) Act 2011 implements the New York Convention framework.6 Courts may refuse enforcement only on the statutory grounds. An award directing a token transfer still requires identifiable control and must comply with sanctions and mandatory law.

25.13 Conflict-of-Laws Solutions for Pakistan

25.13.1 Statutory connecting rules and judicial method

25
Pakistan should enact purpose-specific rules: recognise express issuer or system law for issuer obligations; use an ascertainable account agreement or intermediary branch for intermediated proprietary effects; protect consumers and innocent third parties through mandatory rules; and provide fallbacks based on the closest connection. Courts should characterise the issue before selecting the law.

25.13.2 Regional and international cooperation

26
The regime should support evidence preservation, court-to-court and office-holder cooperation, service and recognition consistent with due process. Provider licences should require disclosure of custody locations and governing laws. Treaty accession or model-law adoption should follow constitutional and legislative process, not be assumed from policy alignment.
27
A Pakistani connecting statute should begin with characterisation. The court should ask whether the disputed issue concerns an issuer's obligation, a contract between customer and provider, proprietary effects of a transfer, an intermediated entitlement, security priority, insolvency, tort or remedy. The answer determines which connecting rule is relevant. Calling the subject a digital asset before identifying the issue does not advance the analysis.
28
For issuer-linked assets, the law validly selected in public and accessible issuer terms can govern the issuer's obligations and internal system questions, subject to mandatory rules. If the token records a share or debt, company and securities law may supply non-derogable connections. A later unilateral change to website terms should not alter accrued proprietary or contractual rights without the consent and notice required by the governing law.
29
For intermediated holdings, the law expressly governing the account or relevant branch can provide predictable proprietary effects where it is ascertainable and not chosen after the dispute. The licence should require the provider to disclose that law and every material sub-custody location. Consumer and innocent-third-party rules should prevent a secret choice from defeating protected expectations. Where no valid choice exists, the law of the intermediary branch maintaining the account may offer a more stable connection than key location.
30
For a permissionless native asset without issuer or intermediary, no connecting factor is perfect. The holder's habitual residence may be relevant to succession or insolvency but can change; the key may be distributed; network governance may be diffuse. A statutory fallback should select the closest connection for the precise issue and permit consideration of control, parties, transaction and protected third-party expectations. Courts should avoid transforming a pragmatic interim situs into a universal rule.
31
Remedies require a second-stage analysis. A Pakistani court may have personal jurisdiction over a defendant and issue an order concerning assets, but compliance by a foreign exchange or recognition abroad depends on foreign procedure. Information and freezing orders should be targeted to identified providers and supported by evidence. Service on persons unknown can preserve a claim, yet eventual liability still requires identity, notice and proof.

32
Cross-border insolvency is where poor connecting rules become distributive loss. A foreign court may recognise the main proceeding and cooperate with a liquidator, yet still need to determine whether wallet assets are held on trust, pooled for customers or owned by the debtor. Records must connect on-chain control to legal liabilities. Provider regulation should require a failure data pack containing customer entitlements, addresses, keys or control process, sub-custodians, governing terms, liens and reconciliation history.
33
Arbitration can improve specialist dispute resolution for commercial counterparties, but it cannot solve every digital-asset dispute. An unknown fraudster did not consent; customers may be protected against unfair clauses; proprietary priority may affect non-parties; insolvency and regulatory matters may be non-arbitrable or reserved. Smart-contract execution should be treated as evidence of performance, not automatic proof of agreement or legal correctness.
34
Recognition policy should be reciprocal and rights-conscious. Sections 13 and 14 CPC supply core Pakistani controls for foreign judgments. Reform could clarify digital service, interim orders and cooperation, but should preserve want of jurisdiction, natural justice, fraud and public-policy protections. Accession to a judgments convention or enactment of the Model Law on Cross-Border Insolvency would require a separate legislative decision and implementation plan.
35
Provider licensing can reduce litigation uncertainty before a case arises. Terms should name the contracting entity, governing law and forum; explain whether the customer owns assets or has a delivery claim; identify custody and sub-custody; state fork and airdrop treatment; and preserve complaint and insolvency contacts. PVARA should review these disclosures without purporting to decide foreign property law conclusively.
36
The proposed statutory approach is therefore modest but powerful: characterise first, recognise transparent choices for the relationship they can legitimately govern, protect third parties and consumers, provide stable fallbacks, and coordinate judgments and insolvency through lawful procedure. It does not require Pakistan to declare where every digital asset is located. It requires courts and market participants to know which law answers each question.
37
Judicial education and evidence rules will determine whether the method works. Courts need intelligible proof of foreign law, wallet control, exchange records, protocol events and custody architecture. Experts may explain technical facts or foreign law according to procedure; they should not decide the ultimate legal issue. Orders should use technology-neutral descriptions and permit identification by address, transaction hash and equivalent evidence without assuming those identifiers prove ownership.
38
PVARA, courts and insolvency institutions should also preserve a standard failure record. A licensed provider's contracts, customer ledger, on-chain addresses, sub-custody confirmations, governance keys, incident history and legal opinions should be exportable in durable form. That record would not predetermine title, but it would make a cross-border determination possible before records and personnel disperse.
39
These institutional measures complete the conflicts rules. Predictable governing law, targeted interim relief, recognition and cooperation all depend on reliable facts and usable records. Pakistan can create that capacity through licensing and procedure now, while any broader treaty or Model Law decision proceeds through the appropriate legislative route.
40
An implementation review should examine whether forum clauses are intelligible, foreign orders reach providers in time, insolvency records reconstruct customer assets and judgments receive consistent treatment. Published, anonymised reasons can then guide contract drafting and statutory refinement. Conflict of laws becomes effective through repeated judicial method and reliable evidence, not a one-time declaration of situs.
41
The review should also test distributional effects. A connecting rule that is predictable for a platform but inaccessible to consumers, or that protects an account-law choice while defeating innocent third parties, is incomplete. The statutory hierarchy should therefore state protected interests and permit courts to apply mandatory safeguards transparently. Predictability and justice are joined by reasons, not traded as absolutes.

Chapter 25 Conclusion

42
Distributed technology makes conflicts analysis more necessary, not obsolete. Predictability comes from allocating different relationships to intelligible laws and preserving mandatory protections, rather than declaring a universal blockchain situs. Pakistan can reduce uncertainty through statutory connecting rules, custody disclosure and modern cooperation while retaining judicial method.
